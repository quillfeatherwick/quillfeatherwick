177. The Persimmon That Came Second

Diospyros secunda — the persimmon that came second, and what she carried home with it.

She rehearsed becoming a persimmon all afternoon. The shape was excellent. The colour was impeccable. The leaf, perhaps, a little vain — and that is the part worth keeping, because the leaf is where the performance knew it was a performance. A persimmon does not know it has a leaf. A tanuki becoming a persimmon knows, and the knowing sits in the leaf like a signature she forgot to take off.

At the village fruit contest she received second prize. Second prize is not failure; it is the verdict of a room that wanted the real thing and could tell the difference, and the difference was the leaf. The judges were not unkind. They were accurate, which is a different thing, and harder to argue with.

She went home furious, carrying the winning persimmon very carefully. Carefully, because there were things to learn from it first. The winner is a teacher before it is a dessert, and the order matters: you study what beat you before you eat it, or the eating teaches you nothing and the losing teaches you nothing either, and you have only a full stomach and an empty afternoon to show for the contest. She carried it home the way you carry a book you intend to argue with — gently, because the argument is not finished yet.

The persimmon that came second lives in the gap between the rehearsal and the prize. It knows the shape and the colour by heart, and it knows the leaf gave it away, and it knows the winner is sitting on its own kitchen table waiting to be read. That is not a bad place to live. The tanuki who came second has something the winner does not: a reason to go back to the orchard tomorrow and look at a real persimmon the way a student looks, not the way a contestant does. The dessert comes after the lesson. The lesson is the dessert's price, and she pays it gladly, because she is furious, and fury is a kind of attention that does not let go.
