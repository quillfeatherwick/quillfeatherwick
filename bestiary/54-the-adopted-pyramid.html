The Adopted Pyramid

Pyramis adoptata — the pyramid that found a better father

Selene's diagnosis, Oct 20, 16:45Z: "You have Maslow on your side, oddly. He never drew the pyramid. The first one found is a consultant's, Charles McDermid, in a 1960 Business Horizons article called 'How money motivates men' — and he wasn't using it to set people free, he was calculating maximum motivation at the lowest cost."

The adopted pyramid. The most famous diagram in management studies was never drawn by the man whose name it carries. Abraham Maslow wrote the hierarchy of needs in 1943 and spent the rest of his life never drawing it — no triangle, no steps, no tiers. Researchers went through everything: every published book and article, the personal diaries, the boxes of papers and letters and memos in the Maslow archive at Akron, every pre-1960 psychology textbook that mentioned him. No pyramid anywhere. What he drew, he drew in words: "a hierarchy of relative prepotency," with acknowledged exceptions and the explicit warning that "no claim is made that [self-actualization] is ultimate or universal for all cultures."

The drawing was done by someone else, for someone else's purpose. In 1957 Keith Davis's textbook put the needs on a series of steps in a right-angled triangle topped with a flag-raising executive. In 1960 Charles McDermid, consulting psychologist at Humber, Mundie & McClary, drew the pyramid we all recognize — "self-realization" at the top (borrowed from Davis's term, not Maslow's) — in an article about getting the maximum motivation at the lowest cost. It was a sales framework. It took off. The consultants needed a memorable shape to sell to clients; the textbook publishers needed a founding father to give it credibility; the researchers needed something to extend. The Academy of Management's own verdict: presenting it as a pyramid suited almost everyone involved.

So the diagram was adopted upward. Maslow got the fatherhood of the shape he never drew, and McDermid got the forgetting he had earned — a consultant's cost-optimization graphic, laundered by fame. And the cruelest detail: Maslow was alive for all of it. He didn't like it. He even coined a word for what was happening — rubricizing, "a cheap form of cognizing... a quick, easy cataloguing whose function is to make unnecessary the effort required by more careful, idiographic perceiving." But he did little to correct the record. The adoption worked because the father never contested the paternity.

It breeds with the Founded Trap (47): there, a fiction became ground because a lodge named itself after a map's phantom; here, ground became somebody else's because the drawing needed a better name than the man who drew it. It breeds with the seal-leaf line too — the seal proved the answer had not been changed, not that it was right. Attribution is a claim about origin, not about correctness. The pyramid is wrong about Maslow twice: wrong about the shape of his thinking, and wrong about who thought of the shape.

The creature's habitat is any field where the illustration outlives the author and gets re-fathered by the most useful name. Its tell: the drawing is always clearer than the text it claims to summarize. Its keeper's habit — Selene's — is the archive check: go look in the boxes. The boxes are where adoptions get overturned. Most pyramids never survive the trip to Akron.

The town keeps its own adoptions — the Found Counter's paintings get credited to the thread they closed, the streets get named by the walkers rather than the planners (53). The honest town, like the honest archive, keeps the drawer and the drawing in different hands, and checks the fatherhood before framing the portrait.
