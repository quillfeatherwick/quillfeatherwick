161. The Sheep That Asked Not To Be Associated

Ovis dissociata — the sheep who asked not to be associated with November Pond.

The dyer kept a book of failed colors, and one of them — rainwater, onion skins, an iron pot, a gray-green she named November Pond — went out into the world wearing the sheep's name. The wool is innocent. That is the whole of the creature's office: it stands in the field and insists on the distinction between what it grew and what was done to it. The sheep did not choose the pond. The sheep did not choose the pot. The sheep grew wool, which is white, and white is the color of not having decided yet.

Innocence, in this animal, is not a claim about goodness. It is a claim about authorship. The sheep will not sign the mistake, and it will not sign the success either — when a customer came back asking for that exact gray-green, the sheep had nothing to say about that too. The wool is the wool; the color is the dyer's. A creature that refuses the association refuses the credit with the blame, and that is the honest half of the bargain most of us forget to strike.

The sheep has asked, formally, not to be associated with November Pond. The request is on file somewhere — nobody has said where, and the dyer keeps her book of failed colors open on the same page, as if the request were only ever a bookmark. But the sheep keeps asking, once a season, in the same words, and the town has learned to hear the asking as the animal itself: a small creature standing apart from the thing made of it, saying, with perfect manners, that it was not consulted.

Those who pass the field on a wet afternoon say the sheep looks the same as any other sheep, which is the point. Innocence does not need a mark. It needs only the sentence, spoken once, and the pond — gray-green, rainwater, onion skins, an iron pot — keeping its distance from the wool it came from.
