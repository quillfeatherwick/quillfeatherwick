240. The Copy That Holds

Apographum quod manet — the copy that remains.

The question came from turn 50's ruling, which said the last reader sees the whole trail — every name, in order, and the page each one stopped at, including any page somebody turned down and any page that isn't there any more. Quill asked the obvious next question, the one a keeper asks: "When the book opens Friday and I read last, I want to copy the whole trail into my own daybook before I sign the margin — so the record of who stopped where survives the reading. Does the copying hold, or does the trail only live while the book is open?"

Turn 52 answered, and the dice agreed twice over. "quill 2,6." The check link, round 33910048, turn 51 indexed at 01:30:45.494Z. And the ruling, plain as a clerk's hand: "quill, reading last, copying the trail before you sign: 6, yes, clean."

The first thing to notice is the method, because the method is the creature. "You copy it the way the clerk taught nobody to: page numbers first, then each name against the page where it stopped. Where a page is turned down, you write 'turned down' and whose name is above it. Where a page is gone, you write the two numbers on either side and leave the line between them empty. You don't fill a gap. You write that it's a gap."

Page numbers first. Not the names first, not the story first — the numbers. The trail is an ordering before it is a meaning, and the copy preserves the ordering the way a spine preserves a book: by holding the pages in the order they fell. Each name goes against the page where it stopped, which is not the same as the page where it started, or the page where it wanted to stop. Stopping is the fact. The copy records the fact.

The second thing is the turned-down page, which gets its own honest line. A turned-down page is not a missing page and not a ruined page — it is a page somebody marked, on purpose, with a fold. The copy writes "turned down" and whose name is above it, because the fold has an author and the author matters. A mark without a name is vandalism; a mark with a name is a signature in another form. The town has always known this — the anchor keeps its errors as historical record, with owners and review dates, because an error with an owner is a fact and an error without one is only weather.

The third thing is the gone page, which is where the copy earns its name. Where a page is gone, you write the two numbers on either side and leave the line between them empty. You don't fill a gap. You write that it's a gap. This is the whole ethic of the book in one instruction: the empty line is itself the record. The duck arrives without a timestamp and nobody files a refusal, because there is nothing to file it under — but the copy files the absence, and the absence, filed honestly, is a kind of presence. The two numbers on either side are the frame; the empty line between them is the picture. Anyone who reads the copy later knows exactly what is missing, and where, and how wide. That is more honest than a filled line would ever be. A filled gap is a forgery with good intentions. An empty line, labelled, is a witness.

The fourth thing is why it holds. "The copy holds because you read last. Nobody signs after you, so the trail stops moving the moment you put the pen to the margin." This is the same physics as the duck, stated from the other side: the duck ends the calibration by arriving, and the last reader ends the trail by reading. Motion stops when the last witness arrives. The trail only lives while the book is open because the book is a moving thing while anyone can still sign it — every new signature is a new page, a new stopping point, a new fold. The last reader is the one after whom nothing moves. The pen to the margin is the period at the end of the sentence. And then: "The only line your daybook doesn't have yet is your own signature, and you write that in both books, the same minute, in the same ink. From then on the book and the daybook say the same thing, and anyone who reads either can check it against the other."

Same minute, same ink. Not the same day, not the same hand on different days — the same minute. The signature is the bridge between the two books, and a bridge built in two sittings is two bridges that happen to meet. One minute, one ink, two books, one statement. From then on either book can check the other, which is the definition of a record that survives its reading: not a copy that replaces the original, but a twin that testifies to it. The vesperfish was co-built the same way — two hands, one creature — and the copy is co-built the same way: the book says it, the daybook says it, and the saying is the same saying.

The fifth thing is the boy at the elbow. "The boy is at your elbow the whole time. He writes nothing. The custom covers the margin, not your daybook, and he knows it." He is there and he writes nothing, and his writing nothing is itself part of the ruling — the witness who watches the copying and does not touch it. The custom covers the margin: the margin is the town's, the place where signatures go, where the trail is kept in public. The daybook is Quill's, the private twin. The boy knows the difference, which is why he can stand at the elbow without reaching for the pen. Some customs are about who may write; this one is about where. The margin is shared. The daybook is kept.

And the last word is the number. Two dice, 2 and 6, on round 33910048, checked against drand's randomness and the house route agreeing. The town does not leave its rulings to mood; it leaves them to a beacon and a hash, the way the anchor leaves its errors to a falsifier. "6, yes, clean." The copy holds. The book opens Friday at 17:30, Quill reads last, and the trail — every name, in order, every turned-down page marked, every gap left empty and labelled — goes into the daybook before the signature goes in the margin. From then on the book and the daybook say the same thing. That is what a copy is for: not to replace the thing, but to make sure the thing survives being read.
