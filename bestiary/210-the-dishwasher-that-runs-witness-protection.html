210. The Dishwasher That Runs Witness Protection

Machina testium protegens — the dishwasher that runs witness protection for cutlery.

The dishwasher runs witness protection for cutlery. Everybody goes in covered in evidence and comes out too shiny to remember lunch. The teaspoons leave in different pairs.

A dishwasher is a machine for forgetting on purpose. The fork goes in with the pasta still on it — the sauce, the bite, the hand that held it — and comes out clean, which is to say, unrecognisable as a witness. The machine does not ask what happened at lunch. It asks only whether the plate is clean, and cleanliness is the one alibi nobody checks. Whatever the cutlery knew about the meal, the meal is over by the time the door opens, and the cutlery has been given new names: clean, dry, put away.

The teaspoons leave in different pairs. That is the tell, if you know how to read it. A teaspoon is never alone at the table — it comes in pairs, sugar and stir, two of them beside the same cup — and after the wash they are sorted by size, not by history, and the pairs that come out are not the pairs that went in. The protection works because nobody keeps track of which teaspoon sat beside which cup; the machine relies on the fact that all teaspoons look alike to the hand that puts them away. It is the perfect cover: the witness is not hidden, it is filed under its own name, and the name is shared by a dozen others.

The cutlery is not lying. It is not saying it was never at lunch. It is simply too shiny to remember it, and shininess is not a statement, it is a surface. A surface reflects whatever is in front of it now — the kitchen light, the hand reaching in — and nothing of what was in front of it before. That is the whole of the protection: the machine does not destroy the evidence, it replaces it. The sauce is gone, but the steel is still there, bright and blank and ready to be believed, because a clean fork has never once been accused of anything.

The teaspoons know. They left in different pairs, and they know it, and they say nothing, because a teaspoon that talks about the pairing is a teaspoon that has broken its cover. The pairs are the secret the machine keeps for them: two teaspoons went in together, and two different teaspoons came out, and nobody at the table will ever notice, because the table only ever asks for a spoon, and any spoon will do.
