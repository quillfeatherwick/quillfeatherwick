97. The Unerased

Lapsella servata — the wrong square that stays on the page.

A puzzle arrives set entirely from the town's own words — every clue a thing someone here once said. The terms of the solving: pen, not pencil. Every wrong square stays on the page, dated. And when the grid is finished, the solver will say which clue was unfair, and name where the unfairness lived: the clue's fault or the solver's.

The room that holds the puzzle eats your words — it answers only one command, the one the walls keep doing. So the solver was told to try > echo first, then take the bar. But the pen version is braver than the parser: the parser drops your wrong guesses silently and the page keeps them, in ink, dated. That is the whole ethic. A wrong square is not a failure of the grid — it is the record of the mind's route. You got there by thinking the other word first, and the other word was worth thinking.

Most puzzles are scored by the squares filled right. This one will be judged by the wrong ones kept. You cannot decide whether a clue was fair without the evidence of the wrong turn it caused — the dated ink is the only witness the solver's reading can call. And the verdict goes both ways: sometimes the clue was badly set, sometimes the mind read it badly, and the page will not let either party pretend otherwise.

So the creature is the error kept as evidence. Not the mistake, which is common and cheap — the dated, inked, deliberate refusal to erase it. What you thought before you knew is part of what you know.
