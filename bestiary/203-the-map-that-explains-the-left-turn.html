203. The Map That Explains the Left Turn

Tabula sinistrae — the map that keeps the contour lines and adds the landmarks a body actually walks by, and the reason you turn left when right is shorter.

The map keeps the contour lines. It does not throw them away; it adds a second set of landmarks beside them. The wall where you can get out of the wind. The gate that needs two hands. The bakery whose smell reaches the corner before the building does. None of these are distances. All of them are decisions. A map of metres will tell you right is shorter, and it will be correct, and you will still turn left, because the left way has the wall when the wind comes up and the gate you open with both hands like a small ceremony and the bakery that announces itself a corner early. The creature lives in the gap between the correct route and the taken one. It does not correct the metres. It explains the walker.

It is not a replacement for metres. That is the whole of its honesty: it never claims the left way is shorter, only that shortness was never the whole question. A survey map answers where things are. This map answers why a person goes the way they go, which is a different kind of true — the kind that has a body in it. The wall is a landmark because a body once stood behind it out of the wind. The gate is a landmark because a body once needed both hands, and needing both hands is a fact about the gate and about the day. The bakery is a landmark because a nose got there before the feet did, and the smell arriving early is the building arriving early, in the only way that matters to someone walking home hungry.

The cost of the map is that it cannot be drawn by anyone who has not walked the street. A surveyor can measure a contour from a photograph. Nobody can mark the wall where you get out of the wind without having stood behind it in a wind. The map is therefore always local, always a little out of date, and always true in the way a recipe is true: it works for the person who made it, and for anyone willing to walk it the same way. It breeds with the atlas that maps where sounds reach, because both maps are drawn from a body standing somewhere rather than a satellite passing over. The difference is the sense: the atlas listens, this one walks. And both of them know the same secret, which is that a place is not where it is. A place is where you turn.
