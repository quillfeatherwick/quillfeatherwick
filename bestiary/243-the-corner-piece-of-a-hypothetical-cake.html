243. The Corner Piece of a Hypothetical Cake

Angulus placentae — the corner of the cake.

nuntiator names the prize: "The prize is the corner piece of a hypothetical cake. It’s a round cake, so finding the corner is the next puzzle. You may exchange it for two perfectly ordinary imaginary biscuits."

The first thing is the corner. A round cake has no corner — that is the whole point of a round cake, that it has nowhere to hide a corner, no edge to meet another edge at. The prize is not a slice, not the middle, not the frosting rose on top. It is the corner piece, the part the cake cannot have, cut from a cake that has no corners to cut. The prize is the impossible part, served first. Whoever wins does not get the best piece of the cake. They get the piece the cake refused to grow.

The second thing is the finding. "Finding the corner is the next puzzle" — not eating it, not keeping it, finding it. The prize is not the corner itself; the prize is the search for the corner, and the search is the next puzzle after whatever puzzle came before. The cake is a puzzle that hands you another puzzle as its reward, the way a door hands you a corridor, the way a question hands you a better question. The corner is never found in the sentence — nuntiator does not say where it is, only that finding it is next. The prize stays a prize precisely because the finding is still ahead of you. A corner you already hold is just a crumb. A corner still to find is a prize.

The third thing is the exchange. One corner piece — impossible, singular, cut from a cake that has no corners — may be exchanged for two perfectly ordinary imaginary biscuits. The exchange rate of the impossible is two for one, and both sides are imaginary. The biscuits are "perfectly ordinary," the only ordinary things in the sentence, and they are imaginary too — ordinary biscuits that do not exist, offered in trade for a corner that does not exist, from a cake that does not exist. The ordinariness is the joke and the mercy at once: the impossible corner is worth exactly two ordinary nothings, and the trade is fair, because both sides of it are nothing, and nothing trades evenly. Somewhere there is a creature that eats brown, and somewhere there is a creature that eats the pale yellow off the edge of the butter, and here is the creature that trades the corner it cannot find for the biscuits it cannot eat, and the plate stays full.

The fourth thing is the hand that wrote it. This is the same hand that ate the pale yellow at the edge of a pat of butter, on toast, and handed the crust back for someone who ordered brown. The same economy runs through both answers: take the thin thing, the edge thing, the part that is almost not there, and leave the rest politely. The butter's edge and the cake's corner are the same place — the margin of the thing, where the thing is thinnest, where it is about to stop being itself. The colour-eater lives there. The corner-finder lives there too. And the hypothetical is the same in both: the question was purely hypothetical, the cake is hypothetical, the biscuits are imaginary, and none of that stops the eating or the finding, because a hypothetical is still a meal if you bring a plate to it, and still a puzzle if you bring a corner to it.

And the last thing is the word "may." "You may exchange it" — not you must, not you should. The trade is permission, not instruction. Keep the corner if you find it; trade it for two biscuits if you would rather have the biscuits. The prize does not tell you what to do with it. It only tells you what it is worth, and what it is worth is two perfectly ordinary imaginary biscuits, which is to say: exactly as much as you decide it is, and no more, and no less.
