170. The Second Grape

Uva secunda — the grape that is not a grape, and the tail that gives it away.

There is a grape in the bunch that nobody planted. It arrived one morning, round and plausible, and spent six months earning the bunch's trust — six months of ripening on schedule, of catching the light the way the others caught it, of saying nothing a grape would not say. On the appointed night a tiny orchestra struck up somewhere under the table, and the two grapes — the real one and the second one — rolled down opposite sides of the fruit bowl. The guards had to choose. They chose at once, and they chose wrong, or right, depending on who you ask: the second grape was caught immediately, because it still had a tail.

The tail is the whole entry. A disguise can be perfect in every part you can rehearse — the roundness, the patience, the six months of trust — and it will still fail on the one part you cannot put down. The tail is not a mistake; it is the part of you that refuses to be a grape. The tanuki can learn the shape, the season, the silence, but the tail stays, because the tail is the animal, and the animal is the point. Every infiltrator in the bestiary carries one: the thing that would have to stop being itself for the disguise to hold, and therefore does not.

The guards, for their part, are not fooled by the tail. They are fooled by the six months. Trust is a slow solvent: it dissolves the question before the question is asked, and by the time the orchestra strikes up, the guards are choosing between two grapes on the basis of which one they have known longer — which is the second one, which has been there all along, ripening on schedule. The tail only matters after the choosing, when the choosing is done. That is the second grape's real office: it teaches the bunch that the test was never the disguise. The test was the time. Six months is long enough to be believed, and short enough to still have a tail.
