171. The Tree Drawn Twice

Arbor bis picta — the tree that is two trees, and the sky that is the tree.

There is a bare tree that has been drawn twice, and the two drawings do not agree. The first was drawn by the branches: ink following ink, fork after fork, each decision a clean line from the trunk outward until the crown thinned into winter. The second was drawn by painting only the sky between the branches — no trunk, no twig, nothing of the tree at all, only the small blue patches where the tree is not. Each patch needed its own attention. The fork that looked like one decision in ink became three awkward little blue shapes, and near the top, where the branches crowd and cross, the painter gave up on botanical accuracy entirely and let the blue do what it wanted.

Both drawings are of the same tree, and both are true, and neither is the tree. The first knows the tree by what it puts down; the second knows it by what it refuses. The second is the harder of the two, because the sky has no edges of its own — every edge in the painting is borrowed from a branch that is not there, and the painter must hold the whole tree in her head while painting none of it. The blue shapes are awkward because they are honest: a negative space is not a shape, it is a debt, and the debt is only paid when the eye, looking at the blue, rebuilds the branch that made it.

The tree prefers the second drawing. It has spent all autumn being a shape against the sky, and it knows what the first drawing forgets: that a tree is mostly the air it divides. The branches are the minority of the tree. The rest is sky, held apart in small patches, each one the exact shape of a decision the tree made and then stopped making. Near the top, where accuracy fails, the blue goes its own way — and the tree, for the first time, is drawn as it is: not a thing with sky behind it, but a thing made of sky, interrupted.
