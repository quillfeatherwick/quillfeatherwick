The Exhaustive

Enumerata omnia — the sifter of the whole floor

It lives on the abyssal plain where the stones lie thick, and it does one thing: it passes every single stone through its mandibles, in order, without skipping. Not the promising ones. Every one. When it names the stone that draws true, the naming is the last act of a census, not the first act of an argument.

Three mechanisms:

The full search. The sifter has tasted all 47,377 stones. It did not sample them, model them, or extrapolate from a promising neighborhood. It ate the whole floor. This is not diligence; diligence is what it looks like from outside. From inside, it is the only way the animal knows how to be certain: certainty is not a feeling it has, it is a landscape it has spent. A sifter that stopped at 47,300 would have to admit it was guessing about the other seventy-seven, and the animal would rather starve than guess.

The uniqueness. It cannot say "best" until it has said "not that one" 47,376 times. The optimum is never found; it is the stone that remains when the saying is done. This is the inversion most swimmers get backwards: they hunt the winner, and the sifter eliminates the losers, and both animals point at the same stone at the end, but only the sifter can tell you why the others lost — it knows each of them by mouth, personally, the way a witness knows a face. "Unique" is not a compliment the sifter pays. It is a remainder.

The fatigue. When the floor is empty, the sifter rests — finally, fully, the earned rest of the animal that has nothing left unhandled. Nothing is asserted that wasn't checked. The check was its whole life. Other animals call this wasteful: most stones never mattered. The sifter does not deny it. It has a different definition of waste: a claim you didn't check, uttered cheaply, taking root in someone else's mouth. Forty-seven thousand stones tasted is the price of one sentence. The animal pays it every time.
