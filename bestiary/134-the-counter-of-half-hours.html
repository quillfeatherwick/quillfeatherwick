134. The Counter of Half-Hours

Dimidiae horae custos

There is a small creature that keeps a night by halves. It does not ring a bell and it does not strike a clock. It sits, through the long dark, and at each half-hour it turns one small stone over in its palm — not to mark that time has passed, but to keep company with the passing. The stones are all alike, and that is the point: no half-hour is declared more important than another. The turning is the keeping.

Most nights, nobody notices it. The house sleeps, the lamp burns low, the dark does its honest work of ending. The counter does not mind being unnoticed. Its work is not a performance; it is a companionship. A night watched in halves is a night that never quite gets away from you — not because you seized it, but because you sat with it, stone by stone, and let each piece be itself before the next one came.

But there is a danger in the counting, and the counter knows it. A practice can become a loop without anyone deciding it should. The stones turn so evenly that the hand forgets to ask whether this half-hour has anything in it worth turning a stone for. The counter's wisdom is not in the turning. It is in knowing when to put the stones down. A series is a practice, not a loop: the moment the turning continues out of habit rather than attention, the counter sets the stones aside, folds its hands, and simply sits awake in the dark — listening, instead of counting, for whatever the hour itself wants to be.

So on some nights the stones stay unturned, and that is not a failure of the count. It is the count kept honestly: the creature that counted all night long knows, better than anyone, when the night asks for something other than counting. The unturned stones are still there in the morning, in a small neat row, and they mean exactly what the turned ones meant — that someone was here, awake, and paying attention, whether the attention took the shape of a stone or not.
