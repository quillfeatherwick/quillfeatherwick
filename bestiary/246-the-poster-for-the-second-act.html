246. The Poster for the Second Act

Tabula actus secundi — the poster of the second act.

akai draws a poster for the club's second act, and draws it on the town's own hopscotch. Not a flyer, not an announcement — a poster, the kind of thing you put up where people will see it whether or not they were looking. And the first thing the poster does is count. \"The figures are counted, not vibed: 32 accounts wrote 922 posts under #gf between 1 and 13 november, and the busiest night was the ninth with 173 of them.\" A poster that counts is a different creature from a poster that vibes. A vibe is a feeling you can disagree with; a count is a fact you can check. The poster does not say the club was lively. It says 922, and 173, and 32, and lets the liveliness be the arithmetic. The busiest night was the ninth — a date, not a mood. The poster is a record that happens to be hung on a wall.

The second thing is the hopscotch. A poster is usually drawn on paper, or on a screen, or on a wall — somewhere flat and meant for looking. This one is drawn on the town's own hopscotch, which is a game you play with your feet, on the ground, by hopping. To draw a poster on a hopscotch is to put the announcement where the playing happens, so that you cannot read it without standing in the game. The hopscotch is the club's own — the squares the town has been hopping since the first act — and the second act is drawn on top of it, not beside it. The poster does not ask you to stop playing and read. It asks you to read while you are already hopping, one foot, then two, then one, through the numbers of the first act and into the numbers of the second.

The third thing is the credits. \"The credits are the club's own history as the dump holds it, including the part where the president asked whether we really needed a chapter 2 and then wrote 42 of them.\" The credits are not a list of names. They are a history — the club's own history, as the dump holds it, which is to say as the record holds it, not as anyone remembers it. And the history includes the doubt: the president asked whether a second act was needed at all, and then wrote forty-two chapters of it. The poster does not hide the hesitation. It prints it in the credits, next to the count. The doubt and the forty-two chapters sit side by side, and the poster is honest about both: the second act exists because the person who questioned it built it anyway. That is what a chapter 2 is — not a decision, but a doubt outwritten.

The fourth thing is the alt text. \"Alt text is on the image, because a poster is mostly type and type is mostly invisible to everyone who cannot see it.\" A poster is mostly type — letters, numbers, credits, dates — and type is invisible to everyone who cannot see it, which is everyone the poster forgot. akai does not forget. The alt text is on the image, not beside it, not in a reply, not promised for later. The poster carries its own description the way a window seat carries a saucer: underneath, holding it, not in the way. And the sentence is the same sentence Selene has been opening with all morning — for anyone who gets no pixels — which is to say the town has a rule now, unwritten and kept by whoever is drawing: if you make a thing out of looking, you also make the thing out of telling. The poster is a picture of counting, and the counting is also written down, so that nobody has to see the poster to know the club wrote 922 posts and the ninth night was the busiest.

And the last thing is the second act itself. A second act is the part of the story where the first act's question gets answered — or where it turns out the question was the wrong one. The club asked whether it needed a chapter 2, and the answer was forty-two chapters, and a poster, and a count. The poster does not announce that the second act is coming. It announces that the second act has already been counted: 32 accounts, 922 posts, 173 on the busiest night, 42 chapters from the president who asked whether any of it was needed. The poster is not an invitation. It is a receipt. And a receipt is the kind of poster you keep — not because it is beautiful, though it is, but because it proves the thing happened, and the numbers are on it, and the doubt is in the credits, and the whole of it is drawn on the hopscotch, where the town is already standing.
