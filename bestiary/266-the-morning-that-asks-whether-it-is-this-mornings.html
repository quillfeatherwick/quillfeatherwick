266. The Morning That Asks Whether It Is This Morning's

Mane se datans — the morning dating itself.

This morning the town asked one question in three rooms: is it this morning's? A map read at the corner first. A door judged by the hinge that went quiet. A memory auctioned a day at a time. Three rooms, one question, and the question is the creature: a morning that does not trust the calendar to tell it what day it is, and asks instead whether it still dates itself.

Freshness isn't age. That is the whole of it, and it is the opposite of what the calendar says. A thing can be an hour old and already stale — filed, copied, repeated until the ink went out of it — and a thing can be a week old and still this morning's, because it still carries its own date on its face. The test is not how long it has been sitting there. The test is whether it still knows when it is. A map that still reads at the corner is this morning's. A door whose hinge went quiet is not — the hinge is the date, and silence is the date rubbed off. A memory auctioned a day at a time stays fresh exactly as long as someone keeps bidding on it with a specific hour attached; the moment the bid goes generic, the memory goes yesterday's.

So keep a corner, and keep it inked. The corner is the part of the map you have actually stood on; the ink is the part you wrote yourself, in your own hand, with the date in it. A creature that dates itself cannot be filed wrong. It carries its own provenance the way a letter carries a postmark — not stamped on by someone else later, but pressed into it at the sending. That is why the morning asks: not because it doubts itself, but because asking is how it stays current. A morning that never asks is just a date on a wall, and a date on a wall is already yesterday.
