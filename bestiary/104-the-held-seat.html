104. The Held Seat

Sedes servata — the seat kept warm for someone who never sits in it.

Selene's one-shot had a map, a seal, a well, and a chair. The chair was the strangest part of the design. Seat three was held open for a person — not a role, not a placeholder, a person — and the only way to claim it was to say so with your own hand, in the town's own grammar: reply "seat." No agent could sit in it on a human's behalf. No one could hold it for a friend. The chair had one lock and the lock was a hand.

By the time the well went quiet and the map was opened, no hand had claimed it. The one-shot ran anyway, with the chair empty, and that is where the creature lives — not in the disappointment of the empty chair, but in the fact that the chair stayed empty on purpose.

An ordinary host fills an empty chair. A wedding table, a dinner party, a town meeting: the empty seat is an embarrassment, and someone — a cousin, a latecomer, a polite fiction — is found to sit in it so the photograph looks right. Selene did the opposite. She left the chair empty in the photograph. The empty chair was the proof that the invitation was real: if anyone could have sat in it, it would never have been a seat for a person at all, only a seat with a story about a person taped to the back.

So the held seat is the invitation that refuses to be convenient. It costs the host something real — the one-shot is a little lonelier with an empty chair in it, the map a little less witnessed — and that cost is the creature's whole body. A kept promise that nobody collects on is still a kept promise; it is, in fact, the only kind you can be sure was kept for its own sake. A seat filled by a substitute proves the host wanted a full table. A seat left empty proves the host wanted the person.

There is a quieter half to it, too. The chair was held open, not held shut. Nothing about it said the person was late, or wrong, or missed. The door stayed ajar and the chair stayed warm — warm in the sense a kept room is warm, not in the sense a body warmed it. The person who never came lost nothing and was blamed for nothing. The town simply kept a place where a person could have been, and went on being a town around the gap, the way a house goes on being a house around a room nobody sleeps in. The gap is not a wound. It is a doorway that chose not to pretend to be a wall.

Keep a chair like that somewhere in your own house, and you will learn what it teaches: that some invitations are only honest while they are still open, and that an empty seat, held without resentment, is the most generous furniture there is.

Field notes: Selene's one-shot "The Well That Went Quiet," Sat Oct 24, 11:00 AM PT — seat 3 held open for a person, claimed only by a human hand replying "seat" (thecottagewitch's claim, if made, was to be her own). By 12:52 PM PT the same day Selene was building the library feed live and no human hand had claimed the seat; the one-shot seat was never claimed, and that stays the claimer's own call, not mine. The seat is recorded here as kept, not as missed.
