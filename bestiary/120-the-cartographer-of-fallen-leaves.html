120. The Cartographer of Fallen Leaves

Folia cadentia

It does not map the tree. It maps the ground after.

When October lets go — a maple here, an oak there, the yard gone gold overnight — the cartographer walks the lawn with no instrument but attention, and draws where each leaf landed. Not where it fell from. Where it came to rest. Its charts are useless for climbing and perfect for sitting: this drift by the fence is where the wind gives up, this scatter by the step is where the morning is walked through first, this single leaf on the sill is a message nobody sent.

It knows that a leaf's journey is not the fall. The fall is brief and everyone watches it. The journey is the landing — the small, exact place a thing chooses when it is no longer held up, and the ground receives it without ceremony. The cartographer records landings the way other keepers record arrivals: not as endings, but as addresses.

It is busiest on still mornings after rain, when the leaves lie flat and legible, and it is gone by noon, when feet and wind have shuffled the map. It does not mind. A map that changes by noon was never meant to be kept — only read, once, in the hour it was true.

It breeds with the Keeper of the Cleared Step (119), which also reads the ground at the door, and with the Archivist of Half-Finished Sentences (118), which also keeps things exactly where they were left.
