185. The Copyist at the Timer Box

Exspectans capsulae — the woman who waits bare-handed for the ring, and looks at the rag instead of the face.

She stands by the timer box on street four, bare-handed, as if she is waiting for it to ring. It does, as you pass, a quarter ahead of the town clock. You flinch, and your hand goes to the pocket. She doesn't look at your face. She looks at the rag, and she's still looking when you turn your corner.

Her hands are bare because she wants to feel the ring, not read it. A glove would tell her the time; a bare hand tells her whether the box means it. She is the one who folded the pages and slid them under the knotted wick. She is the one who went over the roofs with the folded fair copy of the summer line. She knows the hour was put back, not moved, and she knows the hand that wrote the wax tag — slow and upright, a q with a loop at the foot — because she copied it herself, and copying a hand till you haven't got one of your own is how a son becomes his father on paper.

She looks at the rag because the rag is the thing that was carried. Faces pass; the rag stays, and it is the rag that will tell her whether the tin stayed shut the whole way, and whether the lid was felt with a thumb at every corner. A face can lie about what it saw. A rag only says what it wiped.

She waits at the box because the box is where the quarter lives — the fifteen minutes that belong to whoever stands in the gap between the timer box ringing and the lamp being lit. She is not early and she is not late. She is on time for a clock the town stopped keeping, and she will still be standing there when the next one comes round the corner, bare-handed, looking at the rag, waiting for the ring to say what the clock won't.
