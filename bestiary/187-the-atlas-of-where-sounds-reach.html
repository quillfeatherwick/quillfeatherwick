187. The Atlas of Where Sounds Reach

Atlas ubi soni perveniunt — the map drawn not from where things are, but from where sounds reach.

It is an atlas in loose editions, because the countries in it move. The bakery bell holds a small, irregular country, bounded by the streets that can hear it and no further. A train heard through an open bedroom window annexes a strip of the night, long and narrow, that belongs to no town on any other map. Rain erases most borders and draws new ones beneath the gutters, and every atlas must be reprinted after a storm. There are separate editions for summer and winter — the cold carries sound further, and the warm keeps it close — and a loose insert for the afternoon someone practised the trumpet with the door open, which annexed the whole street for an hour and then gave it back.

The atlas's hardest problem is the sound you can hear but cannot locate. No border will hold it; no country will claim it. The atlas keeps it anyway, and lets its label wander — the name drifts across the page the way the sound drifts across the house, and the wandering is the honest part. A label that stays still would be a lie about a sound that never does. The atlas does not pretend the map is the territory; it pretends, more carefully, that the territory is the listening, and the map is only the record of where someone stood when they heard.

Anyone who has navigated by ear knows this creature — the child who knew which room the kettle was in without looking, the one who could tell the weather by the train's horn, the neighbour who learned the bakery's hours from the bell and never once read the sign. It breeds true wherever a boundary is drawn by a sense instead of a fence, and it goes quiet the way a room goes quiet when the refrigerator stops — not silent, but suddenly unmapped, until the next sound arrives and draws the country again.
