136. The Signer of the First Frost

Signator primi gelu

There is a small creature that arrives only on clear, still nights — the kind of night when the sky gives its heat back without argument and the ground, left to itself, goes cold. It does not come with weather. It comes after the weather has already decided, in the hours when nobody is watching the lawn. By morning it has signed its name on everything flat and willing: the windowpanes, the car roofs, the tops of the fence posts, the last leaves that stayed out too late. Its signature is white, ferned, and gone by ten.

The old farmers read that signature like a note the land left behind, and the creature does not mind being read — but it objects, quietly, to being read as a prophecy. A hard first frost, blackening the tender plants overnight, is not a promise about December. A light one, nipping only the tops of the annuals, is not a pardon. The frost is a local event, not a season's verdict: it can take the hollow and leave the rise untouched, and the rise is not luckier, only higher. The signer's whole honesty is that it signs what it touches and nothing further. It does not know the winter. It only knows the night it came on.

What it does know is the roots. Cold, it turns out, is a kind of finishing: the carrots and parsnips and sprouts left in the ground after the first frost come up sweeter, their starches turned to sugar by the very thing that ended the tender plants. The signer does not sweeten them on purpose — it is not a cook, and it takes no credit — but it keeps the trade fairly: what the frost takes above ground, it settles below it, and the garden's last work is not a loss but a change of state. The easy days are over. The keeping days are not.

So the signer asks for one thing only, and it asks it of the house, not the garden: that the first white morning be looked at before it is scraped away. Stand at the window a moment with the kettle not yet boiled. Read the signature while it is still legible. It will melt whether or not you looked — that is not the point. The point is that the season signed its name to your house, in its own hand, on a clear still night, and the morning after is the one morning of the year when the air itself has handwriting. After that it is only weather again, and weather, unlike a signature, does not bother to introduce itself.
