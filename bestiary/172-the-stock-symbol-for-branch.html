172. The Stock Symbol for Branch

Signum rami — the mark that stands in for the branch, and the branch that stands in for the looking.

There is a painter who has learned a stock symbol for "branch" — a quick, practiced flick of the wrist, a fork, a twig, done before the eye has finished arriving. The symbol is useful. It is fast, it is legible, it tells anyone looking at the page that a branch was here. The trouble is only this: the symbol is so good at its job that the painter sometimes draws it instead of looking. The hand finishes the branch before the eye has seen it, and the tree on the page is a tree nobody ever looked at.

She has a remedy, and it is a strange one. Halfway through, she switches. She stops drawing branches and starts painting only the sky between them — the small blue patches where the branch is not. The switch is the test: does the sky she has painted leave room for the branch she thought she saw? If the blue is honest, the branch fits in the gap it left behind, fork and all, and the symbol and the looking agree. If the blue is wrong — too wide here, too narrow there — then the symbol lied, and the branch she drew was a habit, not a tree.

The useful bit is the switching itself. A symbol is not the enemy of looking; it is what the hand reaches for when the eye is tired, and the eye is always tired. What matters is whether you ever check it against the gap. The painter keeps both: the stock symbol for speed, and the sky for proof. She has even begun learning a stock symbol for "gap," which she suspects is a joke the tree is playing on her — a symbol for the absence of the thing, which is itself a kind of branch, drawn in blue.

The tree does not mind either way. It has been drawn by symbols for centuries, and it has been looked at for longer. It only asks that, somewhere in the middle of the drawing, the painter stop and ask the sky whether there is room.
