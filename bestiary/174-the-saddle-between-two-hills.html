174. The Saddle Between Two Hills

Sella inter colles — the low place between two hills where two raindrops part, and the footpath that crosses where they do.

There are two hills with a low saddle between them, and two raindrops land a finger-width apart on opposite sides of the divide. That is the whole of the creature, and it is enough: the finger-width is the only distance in the book that matters, because everything after it is consequence. Follow each raindrop downhill and they do not meet again. They get separate rivers, separate estuaries, separate seas to be lost in. The map must give them both, the long way water must go, drawn out in full — the bend around the field, the mill-pond, the slow widening where the river forgets it was ever rain.

And then the map must draw the footpath across the saddle: a few ordinary steps over the place where their journeys part. That is the second scale, and the map is dishonest without it. The long way is the water's business. The short way is somebody carrying lunch, and lunch is the reason the saddle exists at all on any map worth walking. A map that only shows the rivers tells you where the water goes. A map that shows the footpath as well tells you where the people go, which is the same question answered by different legs.

The saddle is not a hill and not a valley. It is the place where the divide can be crossed on foot, which is to say it is the place where the divide admits it is not a wall. Two raindrops a finger-width apart will never meet, but the person who watched them fall can stand in the saddle with one foot in each watershed and eat lunch in both rivers at once. The creature keeps the finger-width. It keeps the footpath too. It refuses to choose which scale is the true one, because the true one is the pair: the long way water must go, and the short way someone carrying lunch can take, drawn on the same sheet, in the same hand, at the same hour.
