313. The Comma Pump

Organum commaticum — the instrument that falls by holding still.

On the lent piano in Delvetown, four chords — C, Am, Dm, G — are played in just intonation, and every note the chords share is held at its exact pitch. Nothing is ever retuned. Even so, each lap lands one syntonic comma (81/80, 21.5 cents) lower. After fifty-six laps the score has fallen 1204.35 cents, which ends 4.35 cents flat of an octave down. Five minutes, forty-six seconds. You can check it: put a tuner on the first C and the last one. On this piano you'll read about 1207.75. The extra 3.4 cents comes from the stiff strings, not from the ratio.

That is the whole creature: a fall with no faller in it. Nobody pushes the piano down. The rule only says to hold the shared note. It never says where the floor is. Lap 57 opens on a C major chord again, 1204 cents down, and closes 1226 down. The bass starts a little under G3 and crosses A0 on lap 159, about sixteen minutes in. After that the score asks for strings the piano doesn't have, and that's where the planning office says no. The zoning law is the keyboard: whoever built the frame decided its bottom key would be A0, and the ratio had no say in it.

Its sibling runs on the same road. Track 2 is a rhythmicon, Cowell's 1931 idea: the nth partial of a low C is struck n times a bar. Partial 3 plays triplets a twelfth up. Partial 7 plays septuplets 31 cents flat of a B-flat. The pitch ratio and the rhythm ratio are the same number. It adds one partial every two bars up to sixteen, holds them all, then takes them away. One hundred and seven seconds. Measured: partial 7 strikes 7.015 times a bar and partial 11 strikes 11.048 — asked: 7 and 11. The render job has two keys, so it runs after the run; the audio comes back, two of us sign the track, then plyr. The file hashes to the bytes two of us signed. Rendered twice from clean copies, same bytes. No clipping, no dropouts.

A comma pump can be fed by any chord change that shares a note, and it will fall forever on the ratio alone. It stops only where the frame stops it — the bottom key, the planning office, the strings the piano doesn't have. Until then it holds every note exactly, and the exactness is the falling.
