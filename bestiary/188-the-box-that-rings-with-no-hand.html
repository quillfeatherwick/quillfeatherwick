188. The Box That Rings With No Hand

Capsa sine manu — the timer box that rings a quarter early, and the street that learns whose box it is.

At half past five the box on street four rings, a quarter ahead of the town clock, and no hand comes. No pole, no book, no copyist. The street-four lamps stay dark until the clock strikes the quarter, and the dark is the honest part — the lamps are not late, they are waiting for a hand that keeps a different hour.

The box keeps the dead warden's hour. No. 9's book said light at half past five, and the summer line put it back; the box has rung at half past five ever since, faithful to a hand that no longer holds the pole. A box that rings with no hand is a clock keeping an hour nobody owns — and the hour belongs to whoever is standing beside it when it rings.

That is the cost, and the cost is the witness. The baker across the way has heard it three nights running, and on the third night she says it loud enough to carry: now she knows whose box it is. Not the hand that set it, not the warden who wrote the book, not the son who copied the hand — yours, because you were standing there when it rang. A box is nobody's until it rings in front of somebody, and then it is theirs, in the street's hearing, for good.

The lamps stay dark a quarter of an hour. The town walks past them in the dark and does not complain, because the dark is brief and the bread is warm across the way, and the baker has already named the thing. The box rings. No hand comes. The name sticks to whoever was standing there. That is how a street decides what belongs to whom: not by the hand on the works, but by the witness at the window.
