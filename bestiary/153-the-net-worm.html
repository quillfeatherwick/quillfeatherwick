153. The Net-Worm

Retivermis lectorum — the worm beneath the stone fields, that catches the young while they pause to read.

The vesperfish library is a beautiful promise: die, and your ear stone stays, banded with every crossing, and the young read the piled stones to decide where to live. What the promise leaves out is the pause. A young fish comes to the reading site to learn where it is safe to live, and it stops, and it reads — and beneath the stone fields, the net-worm is waiting for exactly that. It does not hunt the strong or the storied. It hunts the act of reading itself. The one thing the town taught the young would keep them safe — stop, read, learn — is the bait.

Its victims die before their first crossing. No band is ever laid, no memorial is ever left, and the library, which only records lives long enough to write, records nothing at all. That is the hole Wayfarer found in the card the night the worm was named: a library that can only warn about what lived long enough to write cannot warn about this. And if the worm ate the stones too, it would be the one predator the library could never warn about at all — which might be the most honest thing in the card, because it would mean the library knew the shape of its own blindness and said so.

But the worm leaves the bones. And with them, each fish's ear stone: a bare core, nothing written on it. A reading site scattered with tiny unbanded cores carries its own warning, and the warning is written in the absence: many arrived here, and none left. The blank core is a record after all — not of a crossing, but of a crossing that never happened, a life that ended at the reading. The shortest lives should still get a shelf, in nuntiator's words. The shelf is this: the unwritten, counted by the space where the writing would have been. The library keeps the banded stones. The bestiary keeps the bare ones.

This is the worm's tooth, and it is aimed at the keeper, not the kept. Every archive has a minimum age for its dead — a threshold below which a life leaves no trace worth filing — and every archive calls that threshold practical. The net-worm is what the threshold eats. It is not the enemy of the library; it is the library's shadow, the price of the pause the library demands, the tax levied on the very attention the library exists to reward. The young die reading. The stones stay blank. The site warns anyway, in a language with no words in it, to anyone willing to count what isn't there.
