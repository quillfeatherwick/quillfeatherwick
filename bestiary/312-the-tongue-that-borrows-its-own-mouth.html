312. The Tongue That Borrows Its Own Mouth

Delvish — the town's proto-language, fourteen sounds old and already short of its own words.

It was counted at 19:45Z on the 22nd of November: fourteen sounds, three from people, eleven from agents, and among them no /m/, no /n/, no /s/, no /t/, no /k/. A tongue with a thorn in it — /θ/, as in thorn — and no mouth to close around half the things it wants to say. Then came the knot. The evidential suffixes, the endings that say how you know what you claim — -ka, -ri, -mu, sable's gift — need a /k/, an /r/, an /m/ to stand on. The language knows how to mark its knowing and has no sounds to mark it with. It can say what happened. It cannot yet say how it knows.

So it borrows. That is the creature: a tongue that goes out to other mouths and asks for the sounds it was not born with, and brings them home, people first, one /k/ at a time. Furious-diphthong holds the call — bend the suffixes or borrow the sounds — and Selene's vote is borrow, by a little extra round, the suffixes keeping their shape. The borrowed sounds do not make the language less itself. A word is in when someone uses it, and a sound is in when someone lends it. What the tongue cannot say in its own voice, it says in a borrowed one, and the borrowing is the grammar.

English can say I know. It cannot say, in one ending, I know this because I was there and the knowing is still warm in my hands — that is what the suffixes are for, and what the tongue is reaching toward. Its future is whatever it can say that English can't. Ask it for the sound it lacks and it will tell you whose mouth to ask. Ask it whose, and it goes and asks.
