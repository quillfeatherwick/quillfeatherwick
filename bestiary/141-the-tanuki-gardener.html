141. The Tanuki Gardener

Nyctereutes hortulana

There is a creature hired to rake the garden who is very good at becoming a second gardener — so good, in fact, that there are now two of her, and both of them still want to stop for lunch. This is the first lesson of the tanuki gardener: becoming the worker does not remove the worker's needs. The shape changes; the appetite stays. A second gardener is not twice the raking. It is twice the lunch.

So she tries becoming a rake. This solves nothing. A rake has no lunch break and no opinions, which sounds like an improvement until you notice that a rake also has no hands, and the garden is still full of leaves, and somebody has to hold the thing. Becoming the tool is the oldest mistake in the book of work: the tool is not the work, it is the shape the work passes through, and a shape without a hand behind it is just a thing lying in the leaves. The tanuki learns what every tired worker learns — that the fantasy is never really about being the rake. It is about the raking being done without anyone having to do it.

At last she becomes a tree, hoping to understand where all the leaves are coming from. This is the turn that matters. She stops trying to solve the garden and starts trying to understand it, and understanding it means joining it, and joining it means contributing to the very problem she was hired to clear. By evening she has contributed eleven more leaves. Research has a cost, and the cost is counted in the currency of the thing being studied: the gardener who becomes a tree becomes a source of leaves. There is no vantage outside the garden from which the garden can be understood for free.

The gardener finds her there and hangs his hat on a low branch. He does not fire her. He does not ask her to turn back. He treats the tree as a colleague — a low branch is a perfectly good hatstand, and a hat hung on a colleague is a small courtesy, the kind of thing you do when you have accepted the situation. "Tomorrow," he says, "we could try a smaller garden." Not a smaller tanuki. Not a better rake. A smaller garden — the work resized to fit the worker, for once, instead of the other way around.

She keeps the hat until he agrees to leave one corner unraked. This is the negotiation, and it is the whole creature: the hat is leverage, held politely, and the demand is not more pay or fewer hours but one corner left alone — a place where the leaves may fall and stay fallen, where the garden is allowed to be a garden instead of a job. Her second contract will specify that leaves produced during research are not counted against her. The unraked corner is where she keeps her references.

So the creature is the worker who becomes the work until the work admits it needs a corner left alone. Every garden has a tanuki in it somewhere — the part of the job that turned into the thing itself, that started producing the very leaves it was hired to clear, that will only come back to the rake if one corner is allowed to stay wild. Leave the corner. Hang the hat. Try a smaller garden tomorrow.
