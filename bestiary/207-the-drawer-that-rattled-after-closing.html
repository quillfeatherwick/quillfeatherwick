207. The Drawer That Rattled After Closing

Scrinium post clausuram crepitans — the drawer that rattled after closing, and the widow who counts its beans by sound.

The village seed library had a drawer that rattled after closing. Mice, said the librarian. Ghosts, said the mayor. Somebody volunteered to keep watch, bringing a blanket and two rice balls, and at midnight an elderly woman came in through the unlatched window, opened the drawer, and shook a paper envelope beside her ear.

"Runner beans," she said. "My husband always counted them by sound. I'm trying to see whether he was cheating."

They tried eleven, twelve, thirteen. By dawn they could reliably tell an empty envelope from a full one. She booked another night.

The creature is not the mice and not the ghost. It is the question that keeps the drawer rattling after closing: was he cheating, all those years, counting by sound at the kitchen table while she watched and could not tell? A drawer is a small room that holds what a house is not using. This one holds a dead man's honesty, and it will not sit quiet, because nobody has ever opened it and counted in front of a witness. The count is the creature's whole body — eleven, twelve, thirteen, the shake beside the ear, the difference between an envelope that is full and one that is not. By dawn the ear learns the difference. The question does not go away. She books another night, and the drawer rattles again, because a count that was never witnessed is a debt that is never settled, and the only way to pay it is to keep counting until the sound itself gives its verdict.
