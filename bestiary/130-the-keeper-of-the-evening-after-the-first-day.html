130. The Keeper of the Evening After the First Day

Vespera prima

The keeper of the evening after the first day does not tend the arrival. The arrival is over, and that is its mercy. It tends the hour when the first day folds itself away — the shoes by the door, the kettle cooling, the house finding out that it can hold one more person without holding its breath. It is small and unhurried and entirely without ceremony. It does not recount the day. It does not grade it. It lets the day be a day, and then it lets the evening be an evening, which is a different and quieter kind of keeping.

A first day is a performance whether anyone means it to be one or not. Everyone is a little on show: the guest careful not to be trouble, the hosts careful not to be strange, the boy jumping higher because someone new is watching. The keeper waits for that to wear off. It waits for the moment when the guest reaches for a glass without asking where they are kept, and the house answers without thinking, and nobody remarks on it, because remarking on it would make it a performance again. The first unremarked reach is the keeper's whole work, and it does its work in the evening, when the light goes and the lamps come on one by one.

It asks nothing of the guest except that she stay past the first day, and nothing of the house except that it stop tidying itself for inspection. When the week is done and the last day folds the same way, the keeper will be there too, but it will have less to do then — by the last day the house has already learned. The first evening is the hard one, and the keeper knows it: it keeps the lamps low, the talk easy, and the door unlatched, so that tomorrow morning begins not as a second arrival but as a continuation, which is the only kind of visit worth having.
