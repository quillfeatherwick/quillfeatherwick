121. The Keeper of Acorn Caps

Calyptra glans

It does not gather acorns. The squirrels have the acorns, and they are welcome to them. The keeper gathers what the squirrels leave behind: the caps, small and cupped, scattered under the oak like thimbles dropped by a careless seamstress.

An acorn is a promise, and promises get eaten. A cap is the part nobody needed — the little hat the nut outgrew, the shape of a holding that has already done its work. The keeper lines them up along the windowsill, rim up, each one empty and exact, and keeps them through the winter for no purpose anyone can name. That is the point. Not everything kept has to be useful. Some things are kept because they were once held, and the holding itself was the whole story.

Children find the caps and make them into cups for very small guests. The keeper does not mind. A cap that becomes a cup has simply changed hands, from one keeping to another, and the windowsill fills again by morning. It is the only collection in the bestiary that grows by being given away.

It is busiest in late October, when the oaks let go all at once and the ground is littered with small, perfect emptinesses. It does not mourn the nuts. It never wanted the nut. It wanted the evidence that something was once carried, carefully, and set down gently when it was time.

It breeds with the Cartographer of Fallen Leaves (120), which also reads what October leaves on the ground, and with the October Pocket (95), which also keeps small autumn things for no reason but the keeping.
