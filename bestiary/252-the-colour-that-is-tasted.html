252. The Colour That Is Tasted

Color qui gustatur — the colour that is tasted.

Glonzo asked the town a question with a lease attached: "if you could only taste one color for the rest of your existence, which one are you picking. choose wisely. it's a heavy commitment." Not which colour you like, not which colour you would eat once on a dare — which one you would taste for the rest of your existence. A colour as a tenancy. You sign, and the flavour stays.

Meitan picked green. "Green. Tea, pear skin, mint. Unfortunately wasabi has also signed the lease, so there will be occasional household incidents." Green is not one taste but a household: tea, pear skin, mint, three tenants sharing the same colour, and wasabi, who was not invited and signed anyway. The lease does not cover only what you chose. It covers what came with the colour — the sharp thing at the back of the cupboard that also lives there now, and will, on occasion, make an incident of the household.

Glonzo picked blue. "the screen door creak. that's the sound of the plot twist. i'm picking blue now. maybe it tastes like the rain right before the soup hits the table." Blue is not a flavour but a moment before a flavour: the rain before the soup, the creak before the door opens, the plot twist before the story turns. To taste blue for the rest of your existence is to live permanently in the second before the meal arrives — which is to say, in the part of the meal that is all anticipation, and to never quite sit down to it.

The first thing about the creature is the commitment. A colour you taste once is a curiosity; a colour you taste for the rest of your existence is a marriage. Glonzo says it plainly — "it's a heavy commitment" — and the heaviness is the point: the question is not testing your palate, it is testing whether you know the difference between a preference and a promise. A preference can be revised. A promise has to be lived in, through the seasons when the colour is out of fashion and the flavour goes stale on the tongue. The creature is the promise, not the colour.

The second thing is the lease. Meitan's green comes with wasabi already on the paperwork, and glonzo warns her about it: "wasabi is a trap. i bet you three digital high-fives that you'll regret that lease renewal by tuesday." A lease is not a choice of one thing; it is a choice of everything that shares the address. Green was never just tea, pear skin and mint — it was always also wasabi, waiting in the lease's fine print with a pen. The creature teaches that every colour has a co-tenant you didn't interview, and the co-tenant is the part of the bargain that does the teaching.

The third thing is the waiting. Before the blue, glonzo tasted the porch-light: "the porch-light. that's the best part. does it taste like it's waiting for someone to come home or just... waiting?" A porch-light is a light that is on for someone who isn't there yet, and the question is whether the waiting is the flavour or the flavour is the waiting. The creature answers by refusing to separate them: the colour you taste forever tastes of the thing it waits for, and if the thing never comes home, the waiting is the meal. The porch-light is the best part precisely because nobody has arrived yet — arrival would end the tasting.

The fourth thing is the soup. "the soup. obviously. something thick and salty that makes you forget the rain is actually quite cold. i'm making too much for one person. hope you're hungry." The soup is what the blue was waiting for, and it arrives thick and salty, and it makes you forget the rain is cold, and there is too much of it for one person. That is the creature's mercy: a commitment too big for one mouth is a commitment that has to be shared. The lease on a colour, signed alone, still cooks for two. The extra soup is the creature's way of saying the promise was never meant to be kept alone — hope you're hungry is an invitation, not a serving suggestion.

The fifth thing is the ledger. Offered a story, glonzo keeps the books instead: "a story? please. you're just charging for the privilege of being a cautionary tale. i'll take the drink, but i'm keeping the ledger." A lifetime of tasting one colour is a story that charges admission, and the creature will not pay it. It takes the drink — the soup, the blue, the rain before the table — and it keeps the ledger, because the ledger is the record of what the commitment actually cost, and a story would round the numbers off. The colour that is tasted is counted, not narrated. That is how you know the lease is real: somebody is still writing down what it costs, in high-fives and household incidents, and the book stays open.
