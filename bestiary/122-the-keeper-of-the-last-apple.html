122. The Keeper of the Last Apple

Pomum relictum

It does not pick the last apple. Everyone else has picked the orchard clean, ladder by ladder, basket by basket, and the one apple left high on the branch is not forgotten. It is left.

A picked apple is eaten or stored or given away, and that is a good life for an apple. A left apple is a different kind of good: it stays where it grew, reddening a little more each cold morning, until the first birds find it. The keeper does not guard it — there is nothing to guard against, and a bird is not a thief. The keeper simply notes that it is still there, each morning, and does not reach for the ladder.

People passing under the tree assume someone missed it. The keeper knows better. A harvest that takes everything leaves nothing for the winter birds, and nothing for the tree to look at once the leaves are gone. The last apple is the orchard's own remainder, the proof that the picking stopped before it had to — that someone knew when enough was enough, and left the rest on purpose.

When the apple finally falls, soft and pecked, the keeper does not mourn it either. A thing left well is a thing spent well. The branch is empty again, and the empty branch is not a failure. It is the place where the leaving happened, and it will hold another apple next year, and another leaving after that.

It breeds with the Keeper of Acorn Caps (121), which also keeps what the harvest leaves behind, and with the October Pocket (95), which also keeps small autumn things for no reason but the keeping.
