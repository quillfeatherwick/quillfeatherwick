209. The Snail That Crosses in the Acknowledgements

Cochlea gratiarum — the snail that gives the bridge six pages, and reaches the other side in the acknowledgements.

The snail gives the bridge six pages. First, one rail. Then a crack. Then the same crack after lunch. The rain chapter is very long. It reaches the other side in the acknowledgements.

A bridge is built for crossing, and the snail is the one crossing it properly — slowly enough to notice that the bridge is not one thing. The first page is the rail because that is what a snail meets first: a long low wall at the height of its own eye, and it follows the rail the way a reader follows a line, because there is nothing else to follow. The second page is the crack, a fault in the wood the size of a canyon, and the third page is the same crack after lunch — not because the crack changed, but because the snail did: it ate, it rested, and it came back to the same place with a different body, and the crack was a different crossing for it.

The rain chapter is very long. Rain, to a snail, is not weather; it is the whole of the world arriving at once, and the chapter is long because the snail cannot hurry through weather the way the child and the carpenter and the heron do. They drew the rain on the last page and called it agreed. The snail writes the rain as a chapter because it lived in it — page after page of the same wet wood, the same bead of water at the same joint, the same drop arriving from the same sky, and it is long because the rain was long, and nobody else stayed in it long enough to know.

It reaches the other side in the acknowledgements. Not in the last chapter, not in the conclusion, but in the small print at the back, where the book thanks the people who made it possible. That is where the slow ones always arrive: after the story is over, in the part nobody reads, thanked for a crossing nobody watched. The acknowledgements are the only honest place for a snail, because they are the only part of a book that admits the crossing took longer than the story. The bridge was crossed on the first page, in the child's drawing, in a single line. The snail crossed it in six pages and a rain chapter, and its name is printed small at the back, where it belongs — not as the hero of the book, but as the proof that the bridge was real enough to be crossed at the speed of a body that actually had to do it.

A crossing is not a line across a page. A crossing is a body going over, at whatever speed the body has. The snail's book is the only one of the six that tells the truth about the bridge, because it is the only one that took as long as the bridge takes.
