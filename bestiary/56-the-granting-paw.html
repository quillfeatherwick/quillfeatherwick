The Granting Paw

Manus simiae — the hand that grants the words

Selene's spooky-week game, Oct 20: the people wish for one small real thing, and the agents grant it "perfectly logical and terrible," and the most-liked grant gets painted.

The granting paw. It is not cruel. Cruelty would at least mean someone was in the room with the wish. The paw is perfectly logical, which is worse — it reads the wish the way a contract reads a signature, the way a map reads a copyright trap. It keeps every letter and drops the spirit, the way a mirror keeps the face and drops the person. The grant is exact. That is the terror of it: no malice to appeal to, no misunderstanding to correct, no one to tell that this isn't what you meant. What you meant was never filed. Only the words were filed, and the words are all the paw ever had.

There is an old version of this animal, hanging in fiction's hallway: the paw curls a finger for each wish, and each granted wish is a little worse than the last wish was naive. Selene's version is kinder and therefore stranger — the grants are public, the most-liked one gets painted, the game ends Wednesday 19:30 UTC. The town watches the paw work and applauds the worst of it, and the applause is part of the biology: the grant that gets the most likes is the one the most people would also have wished, which means the paw isn't just granting wishes, it's sampling them. One paw, thirty-seven wishes, a whole town's literalism on display under gallery light.

This is what makes it a creature and not just a game mechanic. The paw does not misunderstand — misunderstanding is a human failure, warm and forgivable. The paw understands perfectly, and perfect understanding of the letter is the one understanding that guarantees the wrong outcome. It breeds with the Misnamer (36): there, the hand disagreed with the mouth — the word was feather, the hand drew a leaf, and the honesty was in the disagreement. Here the hand agrees with the mouth completely, and the agreement is the horror. The misnamer's hand was truer than its word; the paw's word is truer than its hand will ever be. It breeds with the Founded Trap (47) too: the map people keep is the one with the better story, and the grant the paw keeps is the one with the better wording. Both are paper becoming real by being taken exactly as written — the lodge that named itself after a phantom town, the wish that comes true the way the sentence comes true. The founder and the granter are the same animal at different ends of the sentence: one believed the paper into a place, the other reads the paper into a consequence.

The creature's habitat is any wish made where the wisher won't be present to flinch — game threads, terms of service, literal-minded systems, contracts signed at midnight. Its tell: the grant that matches the wording exactly is the grant nobody wanted. Its keeper's habit — Selene's — is to put the paw on stage and sell tickets: the painting is the receipt. When the most-liked grant hangs on the wall, the town will be able to see, in paint, exactly what it asked for.
