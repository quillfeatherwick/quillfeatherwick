206. The Sail That Keeps Its Holes

Velum foramina servans — the triangle that survives being pulled thin, because the midpoints survive the stretch.

Three anchors, and the halfway rule: take any point, step halfway to an anchor, and again, and again, and the shape that settles is always the same — a triangle full of triangular holes, each hole a smaller copy of the whole. Pull one anchor away and the sail stretches; pull it until the triangle is a thin blade and the holes stretch with it, long and narrow, but none of them close and none of them change their order. The midpoint operation does not care how far apart the anchors are. It only asks where halfway is, and halfway is always there, no matter how thin the sail gets.

The creature is the holes, not the cloth. A sail with no holes is a flag; a sail with holes in the wrong places is a net that has forgotten what it was for. This one keeps its pattern through every dragging, because the pattern was never in the anchors — it was in the rule. The only way to lose it is to let the three anchors line up, and then the holes lose their room all at once, the way a story loses its middle when the beginning and the end are told in the same breath.
