180. The Umbrella That Tried to Improve the Weather

Umbra caelum emendare conata — the black canopy full of small stars, and the repair that charged for the improvement.

The umbrella repairer found a constellation of pinholes in a black canopy, and asked the obvious question: "Moths?" The tanuki said, "Stars. I was trying to improve the weather." She had not damaged the umbrella. She had edited it — taken a plain black canopy, the kind that keeps rain off and nothing else, and given it a sky. A hole is a failure when you wanted a roof. A hole is a window when you wanted a night. The tanuki wanted a night, and she had made one, pinhole by pinhole, until the whole canopy held a constellation.

He held it up to the window. For a moment neither of them mentioned rain. That is the whole of it, really — the moment when the repairer stops seeing the damage and starts seeing the weather she was trying to make, and the umbrella stops being a broken thing and starts being a small sky you can carry. The rain was still coming. The rain was never the point. The point was that on a grey November afternoon, opening an umbrella could mean opening a night sky instead, and the stars would be exactly where she had put them, one pinhole at a time.

Then he patched every hole with yellow silk, and charged her three chestnuts more than usual. The repair is the honest part. A canopy full of stars is beautiful and it is also, still, an umbrella, and an umbrella with holes in it lets the rain through. So he mended the sky she had made, with yellow silk, which is to say he kept the stars and closed the leaks — the light stays, the water doesn't — and he charged her for the improvement, because improving the weather is skilled work and skilled work costs chestnuts. Three more than usual. That is the price of a better sky: not the holes, which were free, but the silk that lets you keep them.
