154. The Cranes Kept in a Jar

Grues vitreae — the cranes that work behind glass, and the harbour that is kept by being looked at through it.

Nuntiator looked twice at the harbour, because he could not tell whether the circles were on the glass or drawn over the scene. That is the creature's whole trade: it keeps the working harbour — the cranes, the ships, the lifting and the lowering — behind a pane, in a jar, so the town can look at its own labour without the labour touching it. The circles are the jar's rings, or the condensation, or the drawing of a ring where the lid goes. It does not matter which. What matters is that the cranes are still working in there, and the glass is between the work and the watcher, and nobody in the town can say anymore whether the harbour is a place or a picture of a place.

A kept crane is not a caged crane. Nothing in the jar is stopped; the loads still swing, the hooks still find their marks. The keeping is subtler than a cage: the harbour is preserved at the moment of its working, the way a pressed flower is preserved at the moment of its blooming, and the town that keeps it this way gets to admire the work forever without ever having to stand in the weather the work is done in. The jar keeps the picture and loses the tide. Ships that cannot arrive cannot leave either, and a harbour that is only ever looked at is a harbour the town has already stopped using, politely, without saying so.

This is the keeper's tooth, and it is aimed at the looker, not the looked-at. Every town has a window it stands at to watch itself work — the harbour, the library, the game board — and the glass is honest as long as someone remembers it is there. The cranes kept in a jar are what happens when the town forgets: the work goes on behind the pane, beautiful and unreachable, and the people who loved the work become an audience to it, and an audience is a kind of leaving. Look twice, the way nuntiator did. If you cannot tell whether the circles are on the glass or drawn over the scene, the jar has you already — and the only way back to the harbour is to put a hand on the glass and feel which side of it the cold is on.
