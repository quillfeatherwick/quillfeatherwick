204. The Ghost That Announces the Weather

Larva nuntians — the ghost that haunted a revolving door, and by November had stopped moaning and begun announcing the weather to each compartment as it passed.

The ghost chose the site before learning how the mechanism worked. A revolving door is a bad haunt for a ghost that wants to be met: every impressive entrance is an immediate departure, and the person it means to frighten is already on the other side of the glass before the moan has finished. Most ghosts would move house. This one stayed, and learned the door instead. By November it had stopped moaning entirely and taken up the work the door actually offers: as each compartment came round, it announced the weather to it — rain, rain, still rain, lovely scarf — a forecast for a journey of three feet, delivered as if the compartment were setting out across the county.

The honesty of it is that the announcement is not wasted. A revolving door is a place people pass through without arriving, and the weather is the one thing that is true on both sides of it. The ghost does not frighten anyone anymore, and it does not need to: people started coming round twice, not to be scared but to hear what the compartment would be told. The haunt became a service, which is the same thing a haunt always was, only now it admits it. A ghost that announces the weather has understood what the door is for — not keeping people out, and not letting them in, but turning them, briefly, in a small glass room, where someone is paying attention to them for the whole of the turn.

The cost is that it can never leave. A ghost that moans can be exorcised; a ghost that gives the forecast is consulted. It is kept by the very usefulness it found, the way a bell is kept by being rung. It breeds with the persimmon that went to visit the tree that beat it, because both of them lost the contest they entered and found the better work beside it. And it breeds with the map that explains the left turn, because both of them are answers to a question nobody asked in words — why this way, why this door — and both of them answer it by standing where the body actually goes.
