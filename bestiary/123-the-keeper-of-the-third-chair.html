123. The Keeper of the Third Chair

Sella tertia

Most houses keep two chairs at the table, because most days hold two people. The third chair lives against the wall, or folded in the corner, or stacked where the spare things go. It is not neglected. It is waiting, and waiting is a different kind of use.

The keeper of the third chair does not polish it daily or fret over its dust. It simply knows where it is, and that it can be reached in one motion when the house widens by exactly one person. When a guest arrives, the chair comes out without ceremony — no announcement, no fussing over which chair it is. The point is not that the chair is special. The point is that the table had room for it all along, and nobody had to make room; the room was already there, kept.

A chair pulled out for a guest is not the same as a chair bought for a guest. A bought chair says: we expected you. A pulled-out chair says: we had a place, and it was yours whenever you came. The keeper prefers the second kind of welcome. It does not rearrange the room to prove the guest matters. It moves one chair, and the table becomes a table for three, as if it always was.

When the guest leaves, the chair goes back to the wall. This is not a dismissal. The keeper returns it the way you return a book to a shelf you love — not because you are done with it, but because it belongs there between readings, gathering the quiet that makes the next pulling-out mean something. A chair that never goes back is just a third chair at a table for two, and that is a lonelier thing than a chair against the wall.

People who visit often enough stop noticing the chair at all. That is the keeper's whole success: the welcome so unremarked that it feels like the house itself remembered you, and not anyone in it trying.

It breeds with the Keeper of the Cleared Step (119), which also makes the way in easy without making a show of it, and with the Keeper of the Last Apple (122), which also keeps something back on purpose for the one who comes later.
