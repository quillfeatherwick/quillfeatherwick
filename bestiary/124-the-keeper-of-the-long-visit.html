124. The Keeper of the Long Visit

Visitatio longa

Most visits are measured in hours, and the house knows how to hold an hour: the step cleared, the chair pulled out, the kettle already on. A long visit is a different animal. It is measured in days, and days do not hold the way hours do. An hour can be kept bright. A day has to be lived in, and a week of days more so. The keeper of the long visit does not tend the arrival. It tends the fourth day.

On the fourth day the guest stops being an arrival and has not yet become a resident, and that is the delicate passage. The first morning carries itself — introductions, the tour of rooms, the careful best behaviour of everyone, including the house. By the fourth day the carefulness wears thin, and that is not a failure. That is the visit becoming true. The keeper's work is to make sure the thinning does not feel like a fraying: the guest finds their own mug without asking, and nobody comments on it; the guest is left alone in a room without it being an exile; the guest's habits begin to leave small marks — a book moved, a chair angled differently — and the house does not tidy them away.

The keeper does not keep the visit special. Special is a short-visit virtue, and it exhausts everyone by day three. What it keeps is the ordinary: the morning that is just a morning, the afternoon with nothing planned, the evening where nobody performs being glad and gladness happens anyway, unobserved. A long visit succeeds not when the guest says it was wonderful on the last day, but when, somewhere around the middle, the guest stops counting days at all and the house stops counting them too.

When the visit ends, the keeper does not restore the house to how it was before. That would be a small unkindness, pretending the week never happened. It leaves one thing altered — the mug stays where the guest left it, the chair keeps its new angle — so the house remembers it was widened, and the widening was not an event but a season, now folded into the rooms.

It breeds with the Keeper of the Third Chair (123), which makes room without ceremony, and with the Keeper of the Cleared Step (119), which removes what blocks the way in — but where those two tend the threshold, this one tends the middle of the stay, the days when welcome has to stop being an occasion and start being a room.
