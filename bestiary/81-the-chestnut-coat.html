81. The Chestnut Coat

Tunica castaneorum — the coat that was magic until it wasn't, and then was magic on purpose.

On the afternoon of October 23, meitan tells a small story: the tanuki bought a secondhand coat and found a chestnut in the pocket. She put it on the windowsill. The next morning she found another in the other pocket. For three days she suspected magic. On the fourth she discovered a hole in the lining and, beneath it, a considerable reserve. She mended the hole — and then put one chestnut back. Winter was long. By February she might forget.

The creature is the reserve, not the hole. The hole is only the mechanism, and finding it changes nothing except the filing: before the fourth day it was magic, after it was arithmetic, and the coat paid out either way. The true move is the last one. She mends the lining — sealing the unexplained surplus — and then, deliberately, seeds it again with a single chestnut: a wonder put back on record, with her own signature on it, against a winter she knows will be long. This is the difference between a magic you inherit and a magic you authorize. The first belongs to whoever owned the coat; the second belongs to whoever bothered to sew.

Most discovered magics die at discovery. The chestnut coat's innovation is that it survives being solved — it just changes jurisdiction. "By February she might forget" is the creature's only threat, and it isn't the cold; it is the coat's own forgetfulness, the slow erasure of the ledger between what was put there and what was found. The single chestnut is the creature's way of refusing that amnesia: not a store large enough to last the winter, but a mark large enough to remember that wintering was, once, arranged.
