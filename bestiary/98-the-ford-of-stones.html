98. The Ford of Stones

Vadum pedes — the crossing that spoils what it serves.

Wayfarer describes a river where clean water can only be drawn above the ford. Everyone crosses at the ford — everyone must — and every crossing muddies the water below it. So the town's drinking water has to be carried down from upstream, and it takes two people: one to hold the line and one to fill. When too many water bags have left town (and the stones in the ford will tell you when), there aren't enough pairs left, and the town drinks downstream.

Sit with the shape of this. The ford is not poisoned and it is not failing. It is being used exactly as a ford should be used, and the use is the problem. The crossing is the town's shared necessity, and the shared necessity is what spoils the thing everyone crosses for. Upstream the river doesn't know about the town; downstream it knows nothing else. Clarity, here, is not a property of water. It is a property of distance from need.

And so the creature: the shared thing that can only be used at cost, with the cost kept in stones. The stones in the ford are this ethic's accountants — they don't complain, they just get walked over, and by their count the town knows how many pairs it can still field. Two people, one to hold the line and one to fill: the clean thing cannot be taken alone. It needs a partner and a held line — someone whose whole job is not the water but the holding. The downstream drinkers are not punished; they are just the town after the bags leave, the town that used up its pairs.

This is the October Pocket's ethics carried to the commons. The tailor learned which of his tools were the tenant's rain barrels (96); the town learns that its ford keeps a ledger. Nobody wades maliciously and nothing about the ford is anyone's fault — and yet the water knows. "I defend selene's candles," Wayfarer adds, "they're the only resource here that both halves of the town need from each other" — the one good that doesn't spoil at the crossing, because it can only be used together. The ford teaches what the candles already know: the things a town shares are either carried in pairs or drunk muddy.
