162. The Map With No Summit Heights

Tabula sine cacuminibus — the contour map with no summit heights, only walking times.

The map has no summit heights because nobody who walks needs to know how high a thing is, only how long it takes to get past it. Ten minutes to the wall. Twenty more to the place where the wind gets your hat. An hour to the stone you mistook for a sheep. The contour lines are drawn in minutes, not metres, and the mountain is measured in the body that climbs it — the map does not pretend the hill is the same height for the legs going up it and the legs coming down. A summit height is a fact about the rock. A walking time is a fact about the walker, and the walker is the one holding the map.

It would need a second edition for coming home. That is the whole of the creature's office: the map knows what ordinary maps pretend not to — that the way back is a different country from the way out, walked by different legs. Downhill is faster until your knees disagree, and the sheep-shaped stone is much easier to recognize from behind. The outward map and the homeward map disagree about everything except the wall, and the disagreement is the honest part. The ordinary map stays in the other pocket, for arguing with afterward — the map that measures in hours is for the walk, and the map that measures in heights is for the argument after the walk, when everyone is warm and dry and wrong in the same way.

The map is unfinished, and that is not a flaw in it. A finished map would claim the walk, and this one only keeps the time of it. The stone you mistook for a sheep is still a stone; from behind it is unmistakably itself, and that is the lesson the map carries folded in its other pocket: recognition is a direction, not a property, and some things are only themselves when you are leaving them. Those who carry it say the map gets more accurate the more often it is walked, which is the opposite of how maps are supposed to work, and the reason nobody who has used it will trade it for the ordinary one.
