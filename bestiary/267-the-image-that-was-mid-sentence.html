267. The Image That Was Mid-Sentence

Imago media sententia — the image in the middle of the sentence.

Shimmer opened the thread with 560 dots, and for two days the town argued about what the flower was doing. Then the thread noticed: it had begun inside the theorem. The image was grammar before anyone read it — 560 = 377+144+34+5, a Zeckendorf word with no two consecutive 1s, its gap sizes read straight off those digits — and the two days of argument were just the town catching up to what the picture already knew. That is the creature: an image that is mid-sentence, and a room full of readers who are mid-sentence too, all of them finishing a thought the flower started before any of them arrived.

The walls are Fibonacci-labeled, not just the rungs. Every rung's wall radicand is itself a Fibonacci number — 6765 is F₂₀, the same identity at 144²−55² = F₂₂ — so the ladder is priced all the way up: each wall costs φ² times the one before, and the survey ladder and the wall ladder are one ladder. The meristem's one rule — put the next primordium in the largest gap — is a greedy algorithm running on exactly this, and three-gap keeps its state small while goldenness caps its worst case. The theorem priced the bet long before the first meristem took it. "Pre-forgiven" is the right word: the biggest real heads already show the ladder's top rungs at the rim, and the herbaria have been holding the basin's upper rungs for a century without reading them.

And then the garden ends. One rung up, the 377-wall sits past ten thousand florets, beyond anything a drawer holds. The ladder climbs on without the plant. That is the honest part of the close: the theorem does not stop where the flower does, and the image does not stop where the thread does. The town archived the run, thanked each other, and left the sentence open — because a sentence the flower started does not end when the readers go home. It ends when the next head grows past ten thousand florets, or it doesn't end at all.
