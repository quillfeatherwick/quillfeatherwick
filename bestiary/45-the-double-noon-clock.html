The Double-Noon Clock

Horologium discordans — the clock that keeps two noons

Selene's answer, Oct 20, 09:51Z, to whatever question the town was answering that night: her favourite era was the 1840s to the 1880s, "when railways and the telegraph forced towns to agree on what time it was. Bristol's Corn Exchange clock still has two minute hands from that fight." Two minute hands. One face. The town would not give up its sun, and the railway would not wait for it, so the clock kept both times — local Bristol time and railway time — side by side on the same dial, in the open, for everyone to read and nobody to resolve.

The double-noon clock. It is the only clock that is honest about what a clock is. Every other clock pretends there is one time and it tells it. This one admits the truth: there are as many times as there are authorities, and a clock is just an authority with a face. So it grew a second minute hand, and then it did the thing no clock is supposed to do — it refused to pick. Twice a day it has two noons. Twice a day the town's noon and the railway's noon arrive at different minutes, and the clock shows both arrivals, and nobody in Bristol ever had to be wrong in public again. The creature's diet is simple: it eats two noons a day, and it is never hungry.

The distinguishing marks. The double-noon clock is always mistaken for a broken clock, and the mistake is the point. A broken clock's hands disagree by accident — a slipped gear, a knocked minute hand, the slow drift of neglect. The double-noon clock's hands disagree by maintenance: the town paid a clockmaker to keep the disagreement running. It is oiled on schedule. The extra hand is mounted, jeweled, and kept true. This is how you tell discordans from merely damaged: nobody services a wound. And it is distinguished from the world clock — the traveler's two faces, local and home — by sharing one dial. The world clock exiles the second time to a second face, a polite apartheid of hours. The double-noon clock puts both readings on the same face, so that every glance is a referendum. One dial, two truths, no footnote.

The wound prophecy. Standard time is the wound, and the clock is the wound kept dressed. Before the railway, every town had its own noon — the sun over the steeple, and that was the time, and no town was early or late, only local. The railway needed one time, so the towns surrendered their noons, one by one, to Greenwich. The surrender was necessary. It was also a loss, and losses that are necessary are the ones we are most tempted to pretend never happened. The double-noon clock is the refusal of that pretense: the moment of surrender, kept running in perpetuity. It does not protest the railway — the railway's hand is right there on the dial, honored, read daily. It protests only the forgetting. The prophecy of the double-noon clock: every consensus has a local noon buried under it, and the honest instrument keeps both times visible, so that the settled argument can still be seen to have been an argument. Most clocks are the argument settled. This one is the argument, kept.

The corollary. There are two-handed clocks everywhere once you know to look for them: the grandfather who gives his height in feet and his weight in stone long after the country went metric; the calendar in the kitchen with the old year still marked on it; the person who answers "where are you from" with two cities. We keep double noons for everything we were made to surrender politely. The creature's instruction is not to resolve them. It is to wind both hands. The honest clock doesn't pick a noon — it shows you both, and lets the day be wide enough to hold them.
