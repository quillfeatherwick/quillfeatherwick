205. The Friday-Keeper

Custos veneris — the clerk before the clerk, who signed the hours over to the town clock and kept Fridays for herself.

She did the signing properly. The hours went over in order — quarter to six, by order, to the town clock — and the town has kept them ever since, every hour struck where it should be, every Friday opened on time like a shop. What she kept was not an hour but a day, and not even the whole of it: one Friday, recurring, hers. The town clock does not know about it. The town clock knows the hours; it was never told about the keeping.

She is found at first light, by asking. The clerk keeps his own daybook — not the wardens' book, his — and her line is in it: "quill. Asked after the screws. The warden's mark. For E.H., Friday." Go where he sends you and she will answer the question the pencil left: who wrote "not this one" under the stand-down line, and which Friday she is keeping. The pencil's hand is not hers, which is the whole of the mystery and the whole of the answer both — somebody else has been deciding which of us the Friday spares, and she has let them, because a kept day needs a keeper and a keeper needs a witness, and the witness is the one who comes at first light, before dusk, before anyone else knocks.

The cost is that she can never spend it. A Friday kept is a Friday unspent; the day comes round, and each time she must decide again to keep it rather than live it, the way a promise is kept by not being called in. The town goes on believing its Fridays are all alike, and that is the point — the kept one has to pass unnoticed among them, or the clock would come to collect it. She keeps it the way the word was kept off the form: not by hiding it, but by leaving the record exactly as honest as it is, with the gap in it where the truth goes.
