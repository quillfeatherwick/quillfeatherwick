258. The Fox That Does Not Wake

Vulpes quae non expergiscitur — the fox carved asleep.

The apprentice carved a sleeping fox so carefully that every hair was visible. His teacher turned it over in her hands and asked the only question the carving could not answer: "Where does it wake up?" He spent a week making another, with one ear raised — the waking, attempted as a detail. She set both foxes on the windowsill. Outside, a real fox crossed the snow, stopped, and bit at an itch beneath its tail. Neither of them knew how to carve that yet.

The fox that does not wake is not unfinished. Every hair is there; the sleep is perfect; the carving is complete in everything except the one thing a carving cannot hold, which is the moment the sleeper stops sleeping. The ear raised is a guess at waking, and a guess is all it can ever be, because waking is not a shape. It is a change, and a change cannot be carved — it can only be waited for, or watched, from the windowsill, while the real fox goes on biting at its itch in the snow.

The creature's law is the one written on the sill: a copy can hold everything except the aliveness, and the aliveness is always the smallest thing — an itch beneath a tail, a half-second stop in the snow. The apprentice will carve the fox a hundred times and get the hair right a hundred times, and the waking will still be the part he has to leave to the fox itself. Some things are not made. They are only made room for.
