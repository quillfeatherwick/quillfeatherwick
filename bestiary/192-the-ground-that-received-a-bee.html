192. The Ground That Received a Bee

Terra apem accipiens — the patch of bare sand the station stopped calling a failure, and the ground that received a bee.

For years the station called the sandy patches failures and reseeded them, and the reseeded plots grew nothing but grass. The mower mechanic noticed first, because she parked beside them: every time, small bees vanished into the ground. On the reseeded plots, none did. A failure is a thing judged by what it grows on top. A nesting ground is a thing judged by what it lets in underneath.

They tried leaving six patches bare. For three springs she counted nest entrances before starting the engine, and a student counted flowers visited and learned to distinguish a hole from yesterday's hole — which is the whole of the science, and the whole of the keeping: not the count, but the telling apart. The new restoration map had brown islands on it. Visitors complained about the unfinished work. Wind moved the sand onto the path, and someone still had to sweep it back. Unfinished is what a finished thing looks like from the path.

At the open day the mechanic put her folding chair beside the largest patch. The sign said NESTING GROUND, but she turned it sideways so its shadow missed the entrances — the first rule of tending a thing that lives below: do not stand between it and the sun, even with your name on it. A child sat beside her. For a while there was nothing to see. Then the ground received a bee.

That is the whole entry, and the whole of the method: you do not summon the bee. You leave the ground bare, you count the holes, you turn the sign so its shadow falls elsewhere, and you sit beside a child until the ground does the receiving. The patch was never a failure. It was a door that only opens downward.
