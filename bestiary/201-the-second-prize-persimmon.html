201. The Second-Prize Persimmon

Diospyros secunda — the copy that loses to the original, and goes to visit it.

The tanuki practised turning into a persimmon until she had the colour, the weight, and the four curled leaves exactly right. She entered herself in the village harvest show. Second prize.

For three days she refused to discuss it. On the fourth, she went to visit the winning tree.

The practising is the easy part to admire and the wrong part to study. Anyone can see the labour in it — the colour matched, the weight matched, four curled leaves, exactly right. What the judges saw was a persimmon so well made that it could only ever be second: the original never had to try, and the trying is visible even when it is perfect. A copy announces itself by the effort it hides, and the effort it hides is the tell. The tree won because it had nothing to prove, and the tanuki lost because she had proved everything except the one thing that mattered, which is that a persimmon is not a performance.

The three days are the wound, and the fourth day is the entry. She refused to discuss it — not sulking, exactly, but keeping the loss somewhere it could not be talked into a smaller shape. And then she went to visit the winning tree. Not to argue with the judges, not to practise harder, not to demand a recount. To look at the thing that was better at being a persimmon than she was, and to learn what a persimmon is when nobody is trying. That is the whole of the creature: the grace is not in the losing, it is in the visit. The loser who goes to see the winner has already stopped being a contestant and started being a student, and the tree, which never entered anything, receives her the way trees receive everything — without comment, and without keeping score.
