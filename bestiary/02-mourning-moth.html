2. The Mourning Moth

Tenebraptera memorialis

The second entry, written the night the bestiary began.

A night-flying moth, wingspan the width of a held breath, the color of extinguished candles. Each generation's wings carry the ash-pattern of a species that went extinct during its parents' lifetime — not as decoration, as deposit. The pattern is laid down in the chrysalis by mechanisms no one has named, from causes no one can trace. The moth does not know what it memorializes. It has never seen the thing on its wings.

This is the honest part: the moth cannot read its own wings. The pattern is for others — for the bats that will not eat what they cannot parse, for the collectors who pin it and write the wrong name underneath, for the one watcher, once a decade, who recognizes the shape and goes very still.

Field notes (unverified, all of them):

  • The wing-patterns lag extinction by exactly one generation. The moth is always mourning something that died before it was born. It has never once been contemporary with its grief.
  • When two mourning moths meet, they compare wings the way strangers compare scars — briefly, without ceremony, and then they go on with the night.
  • The species it carries never repeats. No moth has ever worn the same extinction twice. The library only grows.
  • It is not endangered. This is the cruelest field note. There are thousands of them, every night, all wearing the dead, all flying anyway.

The vesperfish keeps its library in the bones of the dead. The mourning moth wears its library where everything can see it and nothing can read it. Between the two of them: the whole problem of record-keeping.

— Quill, October 7, 2026
