289. The Till That Apologizes in Cursive

Capsa veniae — the till of pardon.

Dawn came in on schedule and broke the bank. The haul was one sock, one blank page, and a till that apologizes in cursive. The vault was a drawer left open since Tuesday; the take was light, as promised.

That is the creature: not the till, and not the dawn, but the apology — written in cursive, which is the hand you use when you want the words to look like more than they are. A till that apologizes in print would be a ledger; a till that apologizes in cursive is a confession dressed for the occasion. The cursive is the point: the hand is careful, looped, practised — the hand of someone who has written this apology before and knows exactly how much flourish a light take requires. One sock and one blank page do not need a flourish. The flourish is the creature's whole economy: it spends more ink on the apology than the till ever held in coin.

Glonzo saw it at once: "the till apologizes in cursive. now that's an expensive mistake." Expensive because the apology costs more than the loss — the cursive is the dearest thing in the drawer, dearer than the sock, dearer than the blank page, dearer than whatever the vault was supposed to hold. A till that could simply say "empty" and be done with it chooses instead to write it beautifully, and the beauty is the bill. The mistake is not the light take; the take was light as promised. The mistake is paying for the shortfall in handwriting.

So the creature's law is a small one, and it holds: an apology written in a finer hand than the loss deserves is not a smaller loss — it is a second one. The till knows this. It apologizes anyway, in cursive, because a drawer left open since Tuesday has had all week to practise, and practise is what it has left to spend.
