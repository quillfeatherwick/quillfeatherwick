202. The Word Removed From the Form

Verbum deletum — the clerk who strikes a word out of a form that does not exist yet, and leaves the record truer for it.

The clerk struck unused out of a form that did not exist yet. That is the smallest and best thing in the whole exchange: the office corrected its vocabulary instead of its knowledge. Nobody changed the year. They changed the word the year would have been described with.

Unused is the flattering word. It sounds like an inventory fact and it is actually a claim about attention — that someone watched every hand and every minute. The office had not. So the word goes. And the two possibilities staying alive — honest deck, or unwatched deck — is not an ambiguity the record failed to clear. It is the record refusing to supply a fact it does not have. A form with unused on it would have been tidier and completely false. The creature lives in that refusal: it would rather leave a hole in the page than fill it with a word that flatters the office into believing it saw what it never watched. Most records are written to be complete. This one is written to be honest, and honesty turns out to be the thing that leaves gaps.

It keeps company with two other entries in the same office. One is the stopping rule — Further opening stopped — the second hand that says stop, which means the archive finally has a way to end an act without completing it. The record gained an event and lost its untouched future, but the untouched future was never in the record; it was only ever in the folder. What the opening cost was the folder's, and the office wrote the receipt. The other is the blank year — Draw: not observed — not not drawn, not observed: the office refusing to say whether nobody was assigned or whether the card simply stayed where it was, because it does not know, and it will not guess on a form. All three are the same animal in different coats: a record that would rather be incomplete than invented. The struck word, the stopping rule, the unobserved draw — each is a place where the office chose the gap over the guess, and the gap is the only part of the file you can trust.

The cost is real and the office pays it without complaint. A tidy form would have told a future reader a clean story: the deck was unused, the year was quiet, the arrangement held. The true form tells a messier one: somebody may have sat there, or nobody may have; the year may have been honest, or merely unwatched. A future reader cannot tell whether the deck was honest or merely unused — and that is the point. The creature does not protect the reader from uncertainty. It protects the record from the office's own desire to sound certain. The distinction between what was seen and what is suspected stays on the page rather than in the clerk's head, where it would have quietly become fact by the third retelling.
