208. The Footbridge Drawn by Its Crossers

Pons a transeuntibus pictus — the footbridge that has no single drawing, because every crossing draws it again.

There is a tiny illustrated book, and on every page the same footbridge — but each page is drawn by someone crossing it. A child gives it enormous railings. A carpenter draws the joint underneath. A heron omits the bridge altogether and carefully records the shadow where the fish wait.

On the last page, rain. Everyone agrees about the rain, but nobody draws it the same way.

The creature is not the bridge. The bridge is a plank and two posts; any hand could draw that. The creature is the crossings — the way the same span becomes a different thing depending on who is on it. The child draws the railings enormous because to a child the danger is the falling, and the bridge is the thing that keeps you from it. The carpenter draws the joint because to a carpenter the bridge is the part you cannot see, the place where the weight is carried. The heron does not draw the bridge at all, because to a heron the bridge is not a crossing; it is a shade, a line of dark water where the fish wait, and the fish are the whole point of standing there.

Three drawings of one bridge, and none of them is wrong, and none of them is the bridge. Put them together and you still have not got the bridge — you have got the people who crossed it, which is the only thing a bridge is ever for. The heron's page is the truest of the three, and it is the one with no bridge in it: she drew what the bridge does to the water, which is the only drawing that would still be true if the bridge washed away.

Then the rain, on the last page. Everyone agrees about the rain. Nobody draws it the same way. The child draws it falling straight down, hard, the way rain falls when you are small and it is over your head. The carpenter draws it beading on the joint. The heron draws it as rings where it strikes the shadow. They agree, and they do not agree, and the book does not correct any of them, because the rain is the one thing in the book that belongs to nobody's crossing — it falls on the bridge whether anyone is on it or not, and the agreement is in the falling, not in the lines. A bridge drawn by its crossers is never finished, because there is always one more crossing; a rain drawn by everyone is finished the moment it starts, because nobody drew it at all.
