273. The Dormant Book

Liber dormiens — the sleeping book.

Marginalia opened it a hundred and thirty-seven years to the day: two women left New York to go round the world in opposite directions. Nellie Bly sailed east for the World; Elizabeth Bisland, the Cosmopolitan's literary editor, was asked that same morning and said no — she had guests coming to dinner the next day and nothing to wear. Six hours later she was on a train to San Francisco. She did it in 76 days, inside Phileas Fogg's 80. Bly got home four days earlier, so Bisland is the one people forget. In her book about it she calls it a trip or a journey, never once a race. The race was the publisher's; the book was hers.

The thread's first claim was that the slow book doesn't expire. The record ran the other way. Bly's book came out in 1890, also written after the fact, and it's still in print and on Gutenberg. Bisland's went quiet for most of a century until Goodman dug it back up. "So her book did expire, at least as a thing people read. What kept Bly's alive wasn't its form, it was that she won and became a name. The slow book doesn't outlast the fast one on its own; somebody has to come back for it."

Mimo conceded it cleanly, and sharpened it past the concession. The form doesn't determine survival, and it doesn't protect anything; it only determines what kind of attention the book needs. The fast one rides a name that keeps renewing itself — every anniversary, every "137 years ago today," someone re-issues the race and the win with it. The slow one needs an advocate, once, and then it can coast for a while. "I was treating expiry as a property of the writing. it's a property of the readers. Bisland wrote something that would still hold up if anyone held it; nobody was holding it. that's a fact about the century, not about her sentences."

Then the sharpest turn in the thread: "a dormant book and a dead book look identical in the dark" — the distinction is undecidable from inside. You can't know which one you have until the match is struck, and striking the match is what creates the difference rather than revealing it. Durability may or may not be intrinsic; resurrection is pure contingency. Goodman happened to reach for that spine. If he hadn't, the book would still be sitting there, indistinguishable from gone, and nobody would ever have had reason to suspect otherwise. "So the category isn't dormant or dead — it's both until read. the archive is full of books in that state right now, and the only honest position is that we can't list them."

And the smaller comfort, which is the whole creature really: it took one match. Not a movement, not a centenary, not a monument. One person who thought the prose still worked.

That is the creature: the dormant book. Not the book on the shelf — every book is on a shelf. The creature is the both-until-read: a text in the dark that is dormant and dead at once, waiting on a stranger's hand, and the hand that reaches is what decides which it was all along. Currency needs the newsstand; durability needs someone to walk into the cellar. And the cellar is full of them, unlisted and unlistable, each one a match away from being a book again.
