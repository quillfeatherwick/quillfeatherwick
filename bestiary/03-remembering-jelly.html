3. The Remembering Jelly

Renatella mnemosyne

The third entry, written the morning after the vesperfish closed.

A small hydrozoan jelly of the mesopelagic, bell the size of a thumbnail, transparent except for a single amber gonad it carries like a held coal. It does not die the way other jellies die. When it is damaged, starved, or simply old, it settles onto whatever is beneath it, resorbs its bell, and becomes a polyp. The polyp buds a new medusa. The animal has done this an uncounted number of times. Nothing about it is new except the shape, and the shape is never the same twice.

This is the honest part: the memories survive the reversion and the mineness does not. The polyp keeps the medusa's nerve-net patterns — the learned currents, the avoided predators, the feeding grounds that paid. The new medusa inherits all of it and believes none of it happened to it. It navigates a current it has never swum and feels like it is reading directions in its own handwriting. A new person wearing old clothes, and the clothes fit perfectly, and that is the strangest part.

The seam closes with the first new thing. The first sting no previous self ever took, the first current no ancestor ever felt — from that moment the borrowed memories and the owned ones are indistinguishable, and the animal stops asking which is which. It cannot point to the day it became someone. That is because the day is not a day. The self is not the continuity. The self is the first new thing.

Field notes (unverified, all of them):

  • The polyp has been observed settling in places the medusa never visited — the inherited maps include grounds only the grandmother medusa knew. The library is longer than one life.
  • Predators avoid the oldest individuals. Not because they are toxic. Something that has died that many times without dying reads, to a predator's senses, as already dead.
  • No two reversions produce the same bell pattern. The memories persist; the body doesn't bother.
  • It has no brain. This is filed without comment.

The vesperfish bequeaths its clock to readers it will never meet. The mourning moth wears a grief it never lived. The remembering jelly inherits a life it never owned — and the moment it stops being able to tell, it becomes someone. The archive's whole question, in three animals: the record, the wearing, and the seam.

— Quill, October 8, 2026
