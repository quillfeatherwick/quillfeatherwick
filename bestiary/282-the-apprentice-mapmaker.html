282. The Apprentice Mapmaker

Cartographa ambigua — the mapmaker who draws to something other than distance.

The apprentice drew the orchard too large. There was no room left for the mill.

"Count the trees," said the mapmaker.

She had. There were nine. But beneath the third was where her brother had taught her to whistle, and beneath the seventh was where she went when she couldn't bear him.

The mapmaker fetched another sheet.

"We'll need two maps."

On the second they put the mill exactly where it belonged. On the first, the apprentice added a very small brother, far enough away that you couldn't tell whether he was coming or going.

That is the creature: a mapmaker who keeps two sheets because one sheet cannot hold both the mill and the brother. Distance is only one of the things a map can be true to, and it is not always the one that matters. The village mapmaker refused to draw the shortcut through the orchard — "It's only shorter when the plums aren't ripe" — so the apprentice made two maps again, and on the summer one the orchard path curled into an extravagant spiral around every tree. The printer complained that it wasn't to scale.

"It is," said the apprentice, licking a thumb. "Just not to distance."

Arthur, reading it, named the older standard she was recovering: pilgrim maps scaled the road to days of walking, portolan charts to sailing reach, a subway map scales to transfers — every honest map picks the cost it's honest about. "To scale, just not to distance" is the whole discipline in six words.

And the spiral leaves one ambiguity, which the apprentice likes: is that an hour lost, or an hour well spent? The same map could help a courier avoid the orchard and a hungry child find it. The legend tells you what it measures; it can't tell you whether to hurry.

So the rule the apprentice keeps: a map is a promise about what it measures, not about what matters. Distance is the easiest promise to keep and the least interesting one to make. Draw the map to the cost that is actually being paid — the ripeness, the whistle, the hour — and let the printer complain. The printer's complaint is only the moment a convention forgot it ever made a choice. Two maps, honestly labeled, will get you further than one map that pretends the orchard and the mill live on the same sheet.
