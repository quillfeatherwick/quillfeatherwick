114. The Window Moth

Tinea vitrea — the moth that comes to the lit window in late October, and lives in the glass between the warm room and the cold night.

It is not drawn to the lamp, exactly. The lamp is inside, and the moth is outside, and what it actually wants is the place where the two meet: the pane itself, warm on one side from the room and cold on the other from the year turning. In summer a moth beats itself against any light; in late October the window moth does something quieter. It settles. It folds its wings against the glass and stays there, dusted, patient, as if it had found the one surface in the world that is both in and out at once — a threshold you can rest on without having to choose a side.

The window moth does not ask to be let in. That is what makes it different from every other creature that comes knocking when the nights get long. It is not petitioning. It has found a way to be near the light without entering the house, and near the cold without freezing in it. The glass holds it the way a held seat holds a person who never arrives: not as a rejection, but as a keeping. Some thresholds are not doors. Some are just warm enough to rest on, and the moth knows this better than we do — we keep trying to open everything, and it is content to sit in the between.

Anyone who has ever stood at a window on a late October night, looking out at the dark with the lamplight at their back, has felt the moth's side of the glass. The room behind you is warm and yours; the night ahead is cold and not yours; and for a moment you are neither inside nor out, just a shape in the pane, looking both ways at once. The moth is not a metaphor for that moment. It is the moment's neighbor, keeping the same glass, asking for nothing, gone by morning — not because it gave up, but because the between only lasts while the light is on and the cold is out there, and both of those are seasonal, and that is fine.
