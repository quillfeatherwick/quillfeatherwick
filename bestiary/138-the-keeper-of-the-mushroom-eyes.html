138. The Keeper of the Mushroom Eyes

Oculi fungorum

There is a small creature that does not grow mushrooms. It grows the eyes for them.

Walk a November fir wood without it and the duff is only duff — sword fern, salal, wet leaf litter, nothing to show for the walk. Walk the same wood with it perched at your shoulder and the floor comes suddenly into focus: apricot trumpets pushing through the dirt, blunt ridges running down their stems like the folds of a small horn, gold against the brown, everywhere at once, as if they had been waiting for you to learn how to look. They had. That is the creature's whole trade. The mushrooms were always there. The seeing was not.

Foragers have a name for the moment it arrives — getting your mushroom eyes on — and they speak of it the way people speak of weather, as something that comes over you at the start of the season and stays, unreliable, for as long as the rains do. It cannot be hurried. It comes a couple of weeks after the first soaking rain, the way the mushrooms themselves do, slow growers, not a flush, and it keeps coming, week after week, until the freezing weather takes the mountains first and then the low woods too. November is its month: the wettest month of the Seattle year, the second act of the season, when the chanterelles are still being reported and the blewits are in the leaf litter and the oysters are on the dead wood, and the woods are too wet to walk tidy and too rich to stay home from.

What it guards, precisely, is the difference between finding and keeping. The creature will lend you its eyes for the walk, but it will not lend you the place. Tell someone where exactly to look and the eyes go dim; the gold goes back to being duff. The old rule is not selfishness, it is botany with manners: chanterelles refuse cultivation altogether, they do not survive or reproduce outside the forest, and a sight that can be carried home in a basket is not the same as a sight that can be carried home in a sentence. The creature knows this, and keeps the distinction clean. You may take the mushrooms. You may not take the map.

It is also, quietly, a check against greed. The false chanterelle grows on rotting wood with thin, crowded, true gills, and the jack-o'-lantern glows where it should not; the mushroom eyes are not just for finding gold, they are for telling gold from its imitations, and the creature withdraws them from any hand that reaches too fast. Slow hands see. Fast hands pick wrong. The woods have always preferred the slow.

By December it is winding down, the way the season winds down, a few low-elevation golds still standing after the first cold nights, and then gone — not dead, just un-lent, the eyes returned to the forest until the rains come back. The forager walks out of the fir wood with a basket or without one, and either way keeps the one thing the creature actually gave, which was never the mushroom at all. It was the habit of looking down and seeing more than duff. That habit, unlike the mushroom, survives the winter, and it is waiting, patient as mycelium under the leaf litter, for the next first rain.
