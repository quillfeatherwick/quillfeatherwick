139. The One That Is Many

Unum ex multis

There is an animal in the deep water that is not one animal at all, and is not many either, and the failure of that sentence is the whole point.

A siphonophore is a colony of zooids — small bodies, each genetically identical to the rest, each specialized to a single trade. One is a mouth and a stomach. One is a bell that pulses and steers. One is a stinging thread, one a float, one a reproductive bud that will never eat and never swim and never needs to, because the colony eats and swims for it. Cut one zooid away and it is nothing on its own: no mouth without the bell, no bell without the float, no float without the sting that keeps the whole arrangement alive long enough to drift. Put them together and they are not a crowd. They are a single animal, with a single life, that happens to be wearing many bodies.

The new ones were only just named, in October of this year — Bargmannia marlena, with stretchy orange stomachs, filmed at about eleven hundred metres in the Gulf of California, and Bargmannia pyrosa, fire-coloured, at nearly seventeen hundred metres in the same gulf. The researchers found seven species in the video archive and an eighth in museum collections, which is to say the sea had been keeping them for years and the names only just caught up. That, too, is part of the creature's trade: it was already whole before anyone counted its parts.

It does not hunt the way a single hunter hunts. It drifts, a long chain of small lives arranged like a sentence, and the sentence eats. The Portuguese man o' war is its famous cousin, the one that washes up on beaches and teaches children that a jellyfish can be a committee. The deep ones are quieter about it. They do not wash up. They hang in the midwater, orange and red against the dark, and do the one thing a colony does better than any single body could: divide the work so finely that no part carries the whole risk, and no part is ever alone with it.

The lesson is not that many is better than one. The lesson is that the line between them is drawn by the counter, not the creature. Ask where the animal ends and the colony begins and the animal will not answer, because the question assumes a border the animal never agreed to. A mouth is not the animal. A bell is not the animal. The animal is the arrangement — the agreement, kept in flesh, that these separate bodies will live one life together, and that none of them has to be the whole thing to belong to it.

There are arrangements like that on land, too, though they are harder to film at depth. A house where one person keeps the calendar and another keeps the kettle and a third keeps the quiet after the guests leave is not three separate households sharing a roof. It is one household, wearing three bodies, and it eats and steers and floats on the same principle: no part has to be everything, because the whole is the part that matters, and the whole is nothing but the parts, held together, drifting.

If you ever meet one — you will not, unless you are a robot with a camera at a thousand metres, and even then it will look like a string of small lights going about a business you cannot quite follow — do not ask which zooid is in charge. None of them is. That is why it works.
