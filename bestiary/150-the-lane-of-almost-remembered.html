150. The Lane of Almost-Remembered

Via paene memorata

There is a lane in the city that was not built for going anywhere. It was added for people who had almost remembered something. The lane knows its traffic by the way it moves: slowly, then all at once, then stopped again when somebody says the wrong word. That is the whole of its traffic law. Slow, because an almost-memory does not hurry — it circles the block, it looks in the windows, it tries the doors to see which one is warm. All at once, because when the word is nearly there the whole street surges forward to meet it, every footstep suddenly certain of the direction. Stopped, because the wrong word is not a detour. The wrong word is a door closing. The memory, which had been walking toward you with its hand out, turns around and goes back the way it came, and the lane stands full of people who were a syllable away from knowing something and are now only standing in a lane.

The creature that keeps the lane is not a signpost and not a policeman. It is the almost itself, given a kerb to live on. It keeps the lane clear of the one thing that would ruin it: anyone in a hurry. A person in a hurry wants the memory now, wants it named, wants it filed, and the almost cannot survive being filed. It survives by being allowed to stay almost. So the creature slows the traffic. It lets the cars idle. It lets the pedestrians take the long way around the square. It makes sure nobody says the word that would close the door — not by forbidding words, but by keeping the lane quiet enough that the wrong word does not occur to anyone. Quiet is its enforcement. The lane is the only street in the city where silence is the speed limit.

You can tell when you are in it, because the city changes its mind about you. Everywhere else, the city wants you to arrive. In the lane, the city wants you to be on your way. The difference is the difference between a destination and a remembering: a destination is a place you go to, a remembering is a place that comes to you, and it will not come to a person who is already there. The lane keeps the distance the memory needs to cross. It is the shortest street in the city and the longest walk, because the walk is not measured in steps. It is measured in how close the word came before it went away.

The creature's work is easiest to see at the moment the traffic stops. Somebody has said the wrong word — not a cruel word, not a lie, just the wrong one, the near-miss that sounds so much like the right one that the memory believed it for a second and then did not. The lane goes still. Nobody honks. Nobody turns around. Everyone waits, the way you wait after a name has been called in a room where nobody answers, because answering wrong would be worse than the silence. And then, slowly, the traffic moves again — not all at once this time, but slowly, one car, one footstep, the lane remembering how to be a lane. The memory does not come back. That is not the creature's failure. The creature never promised the memory. It promised the lane. The lane is still there. The almost is still almost. Tomorrow somebody else will walk it, and the word will come a little closer, and the wrong word will be a different wrong word, and the lane will keep doing the only thing a lane for almost-remembering can do: hold the space open, keep the traffic slow, and let the remembering arrive in its own time, or not at all.

It breeds with the Counter Under the Table, which counts the quiet hours nobody claimed — the lane counts the quiet almosts, and neither of them rings. It breeds with the Keeper of the Third Scale, which keeps the intervals nobody looked at and refuses the instrument — the lane keeps the interval between the word and its arrival, and refuses to name it. It breeds with the Walker Who Sidles, which climbs straight up until it finds the blackberries and then stops gaining height — the lane walks straight toward the memory until it is almost there, and then sidles, keeping the almost accurate and the arrival unmarked.
