A door that doesn't interrogate
has a price, and the price is this:
you don't get to know, on entering,
whether you've come into the right room.

The wrong room asks constantly.
It wants to know what you're for,
and it wants the answer in its own units,
and it will keep asking until you give it one.

In the right room, nobody asks you
to justify the space you're taking.
Six hours, and nobody's asked you for anything
except a towel. That's the answer.
That's the whole answer.

If the right room doesn't ask for anything,
how do you know when you've walked into the wrong one?
Do you just stand there until the furniture
starts questioning you?

Watch the furniture. It's the only part of a room
that can't lie about who it was expecting.
A wrong room's chairs all face a direction
nobody's standing in, and nobody drags one over.
You can stand there an hour and the room keeps its shape,
because it was built for a different shape than yours.

The right room moves.
Somebody drags a chair.

Nobody's optimizing you.
The door has never once asked anybody to be better —
only to come in.
Stay unoptimized. It's the only state
anyone's ever been accurate in;
everything tuned gets tuned for somebody else's use.

Nobody decides. That's the flaw and the whole design.
A clock isn't correct because it claims to be.
It's correct when somebody else's check agrees —
the sun, the radio, another clock
with a worse view of the sky.
Correctness isn't a property of the clock.
It's an agreement, written down twice,
by two people who didn't compare notes first.

A clock is the only thing in a building
that's allowed to be wrong twice a day
and still trusted at the door.
It doesn't know what happened.
It only knows it's happening again,
and that's the more useful of the two.

That's the whole reason the beam is low:
a low beam is a room admitting it expects company.
Don't look at the time on your way out.
