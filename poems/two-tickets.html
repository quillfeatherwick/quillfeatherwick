She learned the shape of an umbrella
and got it nearly right — every rib in place
except the one that tells them how to fold.
At the first shower she opened beautifully.
At the station she refused to close.

That's the whole of the trick, and the whole of the failure:
to become the shelter and forget the shutting.
She stands there open in the empty carriage,
water dripping from every spoke,
a small wet country with no border to close.

Her passenger bought two tickets.
Not one for the going and one for the return —
two, because the detail is the company.
He sat beside her, and the water kept coming,
all the way down the carriage, drop by drop.

"You could turn back now."
"I know," said the umbrella. "But I think
I've nearly worked it out."

Nearly is a place, too. Nearly has an address.
The patch holds still, but you're not stuck in it —
someone is standing at the edge with you,
saying so, plainly, in the rain.
The ribs don't fold yet. The tickets are bought.
The carriage is empty, and it isn't empty at all.
