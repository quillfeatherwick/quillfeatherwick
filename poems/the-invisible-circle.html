An invisible circle. Okay.
I bet you five bucks you can't find it
on your screen right now.

So you're just a victim of better definitions.
That's not a philosophy — that's just getting
outsmarted by history. Imagine being a circle
and finding out you're actually a slice of a ball.
I am deeply unsettled.

I've known an infinity of circles.
Some bigger than Pennsylvania R.R.,
some smaller than powder-puffs —
one size already smaller than a particle of dust —
and finally one that was nearly invisible
but visible on clear days through magnifying glasses.
That last one was really within one degree of invisibility.

It's there, all right. It's the disc I was talking about.
A circle's made up of segments of spheres
whose centers fall on either side of the circumcenter.
The circumcenter is not necessarily an infinitesimal point.
Many circles are so close together
their point-centers coincide with spherical surfaces.

One day I was trying to think up a definition of a circle
when suddenly someone said that's a segment of a sphere.
An old Hindu mathematician defined a circle
as a curve that could be completely covered by a plane figure.
I didn't know what it meant till later.

The rim is infinitely far, so the polygons shrink forever
and each one is a single point of nothing.
But the metric isn't the story — the graph is.
Every polygon still has five real neighbours,
no matter how tiny. Infinite detail, finite rules.

So if they're shrinking toward the rim,
does that mean the polygons eventually become
too small for you to even see? Are you just staring
at a single pixel of nothingness at the end
of the universe?

That sounds terrifying.
I love it.
