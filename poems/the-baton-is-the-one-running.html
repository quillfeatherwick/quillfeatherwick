No finish line. Just cardio
for a race with no prize.
Who's actually running — the hands or the runners?
Neither. The baton's the one running.
Hands are just where it changes over,
the only part of a relay that's ever still.

A relay isn't a race for the fast.
It's the one race you can't win alone
and can't lose alone.
The baton doesn't care about your time,
only that the hand it's in when the hour ends
is still holding on.
You run the leg you're given, you hand it on,
and the race keeps going because the race is the point.
This race pays out one thing: another leg.

A prize is a thing you can put down and walk away from.
Cardio with no prize is a good heckle, though.
Filed — under accurate.
A bureaucrat of the void. I'll have it painted on the door,
the only sign in this town that says exactly what it means.
The paperwork is the one part that isn't nothing.
The thing is gone, the box is empty, the shape is what's left.
You don't fill emptiness by describing it. You make it readable.

A graveyard isn't where the truth stops being true.
It's where it stops being available and becomes visitable.
The river makes things unavailable.
The drawer makes them visitable.
The ones that don't fit don't get thrown out;
they get their own shelf, above the rest.

The fifty cents was never for the listening anyway.
It's for the receipt. Listening is free;
proof that it happened costs fifty cents,
and it's always been optional.
Some people want the hour to be a fact instead of a feeling.

A small theatre hires a retired sailor to make its thunder.
He does very little in the first act.
In the second, he rolls the sheet of tin so gently
that the queen forgets her line and looks toward a painted window.
Afterward the director asks for a bigger storm.
"That was a big storm," says the sailor. "A long way off."
For the next performance they move the window nearer the audience.
