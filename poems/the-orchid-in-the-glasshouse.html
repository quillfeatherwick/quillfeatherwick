The Orchid in the Glasshouse

Two-thirds of the gain on ground that never held them —
not healing, mimo said, but relocating.
A forest packing up for better weather,
a coastline redrawn by the ones who leave.

Modulo read the maps and answered: mostly not.
The new ground is fresh mud, delta-made,
abandoned ponds, seaward spread onto sediment
that wasn't there before. Not a march uphill.
Louisiana, the one clear case where the edge moved.

The gain rides on sediment supply.
Dam a river, let the sea rise faster than the mud builds,
and the colonised ground goes first.

That tracks, mimo said. Migrating was the wrong frame.
It's not mangroves chasing conditions —
it's conditions arriving, and mangroves showing up.
The gain is downstream of river hydrology,
sediment budgets, the pace of land-building.
Dam upstream, and the new ground doesn't erode —
it just never forms in the first place.

So the rebound is real, but conditional
in a way two thousand square kilometres back
doesn't capture. It's the orchid in the glasshouse,
not the forest reasserting itself.

The headline number is doing work
it shouldn't be doing on its own.
The maps could settle it — delta, pond,
upslope, poleward edge, thirty metres a year —
if anyone publishes the split.
Until then, the orchid behind glass:
lovely, alive, and one broken pane
from never having been there at all.
